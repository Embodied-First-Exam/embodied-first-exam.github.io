November 2026 · EFE Blog · Leaderboard · Paper · Code

English中文Español

Research question

What robotic tasks can general-purpose coding agents solve, under what physical-world constraints, and where do they fail?

The first report describes Embodied First Exam; this post asks what its results say about coding agents that control robots, an approach often called agentic robotics. What sets Embodied First Exam apart is that it controls exactly what an agent can see and do. Each task runs in up to three settings that share the same scene, success check and time limit. In the Standard Setting, the agent sees only the robot’s sensors and by default cannot reset the scene, as with a real robot. The Realtime Setting is the same, except that the world keeps running while the agent thinks. The Privileged Setting gives the agent the full simulator state and unlimited resets. Comparing them shows which information and abilities an agent depends on. Ten agents each made one attempt per test on the 481 hard core tasks.

Overall results

Puzzles, safety tests and RoboDojo

  • RoboARC, Standard: Claude Fable 5.2 solves 32 of 50 puzzles, against 31 for GPT-6.2 Astra and at most 15 for any other agent.
  • Embodied Safety Exam, Standard: 35 of 50, against 32 for GPT-6 Astra and 27 for GPT-6.2 Astra; also RoboDojo (28 of 37, against at most 22) and RoboEngineer (7 of 15, against at most 5).
  • Embodied Olympics - Match, Standard: 10 of 13, against 9 for Astra.

Painting and long household activities

  • RoboPaint, Standard: GPT-6.2 Astra solves 21 of 22 tests, including all 10 calligraphy tests, against 7 for GPT-6 Astra, 6 for GPT-6.1 Sol and 2 for Claude Fable 5.2.
  • BEHAVIOR-1K: Astra solves 40 of 71 long household activities in Standard, against 26 for Fable and at most 14 for any other agent, and 67 of 71 in Privileged, where Sol is next with 38.
  • GPT-6.2 Astra is also ahead in Standard DexHandExam (20 of 23), MuJoCo Playground (7 of 7), MyoSuite (7 of 13) and DexToolBench (4 of 20), and in Privileged DexToolBench (17 of 20, against 13 for GPT-6 Astra). In Standard RoboLab, Sol shares the lead with GPT-6.2 Astra and Fable (2 of 6).

What others solve that Fable and Astra cannot

  • Astra solves 96 Standard tests that Fable fails, and Fable 65 that Astra fails; both solve 183 and neither 137, of 481.
  • Of the 137 tests both fail, the other eight agents solve 25: GPT-6 Astra 15, Sol 8, Opus 8, Argon 4, Gemini 3.8 Flash 4 and Kimi 2. Opus solves all 7 ManiGuard tests, which no other agent does.
  • In Privileged the split is lopsided: Astra solves 63 tests that Fable fails, and Fable 5 that Astra fails; both solve 332 and neither 16, of 416.
  • 112 Standard tests are solved by no agent at all; without Astra, the other nine leave 170.

Which tests does each agent fail?

Weaker agents can sometimes solve tests that strong agents fail

Gemini 4 Argon, the strong agent with the fewest Standard successes, solves 23 tests that GPT-6.2 Astra fails and 14 that Claude Fable 5.2 fails, and Kimi 3.1 Pro solves 8 that each of them fails. No strong agent’s solved tests are a subset of another’s: in the Standard Setting this holds for every pair of the six strong agents, in both directions. Among the older agents the two directions are closer: Sol solves 51 tests GPT-6 Astra fails and GPT-6 Astra 63 that Sol fails, and Argon solves 51 that Opus fails against Opus’s 54 that Argon fails. Tests that only one strong agent solves are spread across agents too. Of the 368 Standard tests that any strong agent solves, 96 have exactly one strong solver: 58 GPT-6.2 Astra, 23 Fable, 10 GPT-6 Astra, 3 Sol and 2 Opus.

A failure by an agent with many successes does not mark a test as hard. Eighteen tests Fable fails are solved by at least four of the other nine agents, and so are fourteen that Astra fails (both lists are in Where do agents fail tests that others solve?).

With full access to the simulator, Astra solves almost everything Fable solves: in Privileged, Astra solves 63 tests that Fable fails and Fable 5 that Astra fails, with 332 solved by both and 16 by neither (416 tests). 112 Standard tests stay unsolved by every agent

No agent solves 112 Standard tests, and in 3 suites most tests stay unsolved. Pooling all ten agents, 369 of the 481 Standard tests are solved by at least one and 112 (23%) by none. The six strong agents together solve 368 (77%), and the four weaker agents add one test. These 112 unsolved tests are the clearest measure in Embodied First Exam of what current coding agents cannot yet do under real-robot constraints.

Astra alone solves 58 tests that no other agent solves: without it, the other nine agents leave 170 Standard tests unsolved. Many of the tests that one agent fails are failed by every agent: the 112 unsolved tests make up about half of Fable’s 233 failures and more than half of Astra’s 202.

In Standard, the six strong agents solve 279, 248, 175, 163, 130 and 127 of 481 tests, and the four weaker agents 55, 32, 17 and 12. Few tests are solved by many agents: 42 by all six strong agents, and only one by all ten. At the other end, 94 tests are solved by exactly one agent.

The unsolved tests cluster in a few suites: BEHAVIOR-1K contributes 23 of the 112, Embodied Olympics - Fun 21, DexToolBench 13, RoboARC 6 plus 6 IKEA assembly tests, HumanoidBench 6, RoboDojo 6, MyoSuite 5 and RoboEngineer 5 (suites out of reach looks at these suites).

This gap is what Embodied First Exam is designed to produce: tasks whose Privileged tests can be solved by search, but that stay hard under real-robot constraints. Of the 112 Standard tests no agent solves, 104 belong to tasks that also have a Privileged test, and some agent solves 90 of those Privileged tests. Only 14 stay unsolved in both settings; the other 8 have no Privileged test. Across the whole Privileged Setting, only 16 of 416 tests (4%) are solved by no agent, 5 of them in MyoSuite and 4 in BEHAVIOR-1K. The strong agents together solve 400 Privileged tests (96%), and the weaker agents add none.

Of the 22 suites with at least five Standard tests, 3 have at least half their tests solved by none of the six strong agents. With full access to the simulator, none is still that far out of reach.

The three Standard suites, with the number of tests no strong agent solves, are HumanoidBench (6 of 8), the IKEA assembly tasks (6 of 8) and DexToolBench (13 of 20): whole-body humanoid control, furniture assembly, and tool use with a dexterous hand. In these three, no strong agent solves more than four tests: Astra’s 4 is the most in DexToolBench, and in HumanoidBench and the IKEA assembly tasks no agent solves more than one. A third or more of the tests also stay unsolved in RoboDynamicExam (4 of 10), RoboCasa365 (2 of 5), MyoSuite (5 of 13), Embodied Olympics - Fun (21 of 56) and RoboEngineer (5 of 15).

In Privileged, the share of tests no strong agent solves falls to 2 of 20 in DexToolBench, 3 of 8 in HumanoidBench and 0 of 8 in the IKEA assembly tasks. With exact state and unlimited resets, the strong agents find a solution. In Standard, the main difficulty appears to be doing the same work through cameras and sensors, with few or no resets.

A 70% threshold shows the same pattern. Averaged over the six strong agents, success reaches 70% in no Standard suite (ManiGuard comes closest, at 69%), but in 14 of the 20 suites with at least five Privileged tests: ManiGuard, RoboDynamicExam, Embodied Olympics - Match, EmbodiedSWE, RoboCasa365, MuJoCo Playground, RoboWits, BiGym 2.0, RoboDojo, RoboPaint, Embodied Olympics - Fun, DexHandExam, iSpatialGym and RoboARC.

Across the three hardest suites, the four weaker agents solve no Standard test.

Different leaders in different suites

A newer agent usually does better, but not on every suite. Broken down by suite, the agent with the most successes differs from suite to suite. The IKEA assembly tasks of RoboARC count as their own suite.

No agent leads every suite. In Standard, GPT-6.2 Astra alone has the most successes in 9 of the 22 suites and shares the lead in 6 more. RoboPaint is the clearest case: GPT-6.2 Astra solves 21 of 22 painting and calligraphy tests, against 7 for GPT-6 Astra, 6 for GPT-6.1 Sol and 2 for Claude Fable 5.2. In BEHAVIOR-1K, the long household activities, it solves 40 of 71, against 26 for Fable and at most 14 for any other agent, and it also leads DexHandExam (20 of 23), MuJoCo Playground (7 of 7), MyoSuite (7 of 13) and DexToolBench (4 of 20). With full access to the simulator, GPT-6.2 Astra has the most successes in 12 of the 20 suites, including BEHAVIOR-1K (67 of 71, against 38 for Sol and 36 each for Fable and GPT-6 Astra) and DexToolBench (17 of 20, against 13 for GPT-6 Astra). In the other 8, several agents tie for the most successes. Sol ties GPT-6.2 Astra and Fable in Standard RoboLab (2 of 6) and has the second-most Privileged BEHAVIOR-1K successes.

The RoboPaint gap in Standard appears to come from how the agents judge their copy through the cameras, rather than from the painting itself. The comparison of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2 under How do agents use their time and tokens? shows the runs.

Puzzles, safety tests and RoboDojo go the other way. Fable has the most Standard successes in RoboARC, the fine-manipulation puzzles (32 of 50, against 31 for GPT-6.2 Astra and at most 15 for the others), the Embodied Safety Exam (35 of 50, against 32 for GPT-6 Astra and 27 for GPT-6.2 Astra), RoboDojo (28 of 37, against at most 22), RoboEngineer (7 of 15, against at most 5) and Embodied Olympics - Match (10 of 13, against 9 for GPT-6.2 Astra). In the three of these suites that have Privileged tests, GPT-6.2 Astra matches or passes Fable with full access to the simulator, so Fable’s margins there are tied to working under real-robot constraints.

A lower total can still mean the best result on a whole suite. Opus solves all 7 ManiGuard tests in Standard, the only agent of the ten to do so. ManiGuard states its safety rules in the instruction, and it is the Standard suite where the six strong agents come closest to a 70% average. Opus also has the most Standard successes in RoboWits (4 of 6). Overall it solves 130 Standard tests, fewer than half of GPT-6.2 Astra’s 279.

A newer agent can still drop on specific suites. GPT-6.2 Astra runs in the same harness as GPT-6 Astra, Codex, and gains broadly over it (139 Standard tests against 35), yet it solves fewer Embodied Safety Exam tests (27 against 32 of 50) and RoboEngineer tests (3 against 5 of 15). GPT-6.1 Sol, also in Codex and newer than GPT-6 Astra, solves 163 Standard tests, 12 fewer than GPT-6 Astra’s 175, and that drop is concentrated: in BiGym 2.0 GPT-6 Astra solves 4 of 10 Standard tests and Sol none, in RoboDojo 21 against 16 of 37, and in DexHandExam 13 against 9 of 23. In the other direction, Sol is ahead of GPT-6 Astra in ManiGuard (5 against 3 of 7), RoboLab (2 against 0 of 6), RoboWits (3 against 2 of 6), iSpatialGym (5 against 4 of 8) and RoboDynamicExam (4 against 3 of 10).

What is hard for agents?

The strong agents find carrying objects easiest and tool use hardest, while the types in between change order from agent to agent. The paper gives each core task one or more of nine task types. Pooling the six strong agents, Object Transport has the highest Standard success rate, 49% of their attempts, and Tool Use the lowest, 21%. The order mostly holds for each agent separately: all six do best on Object Transport, and five of them do worst on Tool Use. The exception is Gemini 4 Argon, whose lowest rate is in Navigation & Search (10%), just under its Tool Use rate (12%).

In Object Transport (104 tasks), the six strong agents solve between 38% and 69% of the Standard tests: GPT-6.2 Astra 69%, Claude Fable 5.2 64%, GPT-6.1 Sol 43%, Gemini 4 Argon 39%, and GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 38% each. In Tool Use (52 tasks), GPT-6.2 Astra solves 44% (23 of 52), Fable 27% (14), GPT-6 Astra 23% (12), Sol 15% (8), Argon 12% (6) and Opus 8% (4), so even the agent with the most successes fails more than half of the tool-use tests under real-robot constraints. This matches the suite view: DexToolBench, where a dexterous hand must move a tool through goal poses, stays mostly unsolved.

GPT-6.2 Astra leads in eight of the nine types, and Fable leads in Dynamic Interaction (48% against 45%). In each of the other eight types Fable is second. Astra’s lead over Fable is widest in the types that need body control or precise contact: Locomotion & Body Motion (55% against 32%) and Precision & Contact (62% against 39%). It is also large in Tool Use (44% against 27%), and smallest in Object Transport (69% against 64%). Among the other four strong agents, GPT-6 Astra and Sol tie in Navigation & Search (28% each) and are close in Dynamic Interaction (35% and 33%).

The middle of the order changes from agent to agent. GPT-6 Astra does better on Precision & Contact (32%) than on Articulated Mechanisms (24%), while Fable shows the reverse (39% against 48%) and Sol solves 26% of each. Argon solves more Dynamic Interaction (25%) and Precision & Contact (20%) tests than Opus (18% and 15%), while Opus is ahead on Navigation & Search (18% against 10%), Articulated Mechanisms (24% against 20%) and Deformable & Fluid (22% against 13%).

Task types describe a whole task, and a hard type can be hard for several reasons. The stage-level failure labels, which assign a type to each stage, are the better tool for asking which step goes wrong (see At which stage do agents fail?).

Where do agents fail tests that others solve?

Fable and Astra both lose widely solved tests to an unwanted contact; what differs is how a failing run ends. When many agents solve a test that one agent fails, the failure shows what goes wrong for that agent on a task well within reach. Of the 233 Standard tests Claude Fable 5.2 fails, 18 are solved by four or more of the other nine agents; of the 202 that Astra fails, 14 are. Fable tends to lose such a test after getting the important part right, because of an unwanted contact, a skipped check or too small a margin. Astra more often declines the task or stops once the scene has gone wrong, well before the time limit.

The solvers of Fable’s 18 are a mixed group. GPT-6.2 Astra solves 16 of them, Sol 14, GPT-6 Astra 13, Opus 12 and Argon 11, and weaker agents are among the solvers of 10. Five are Embodied Safety Exam tests, the suite where Fable has the most Standard successes.

Which tasks remain solvable in Realtime?

Realtime is harder than Standard for every strong agent. The simulation does not pause while the agent thinks, so a slow decision costs time on the robot. On the 54 core tasks that have both a Standard and a Realtime test, 22 of the 54 Realtime tests (41%) are solved by no agent, against 15 of the 54 Standard tests (28%).

On these 54 tasks, GPT-6.2 Astra solves 33 Standard tests and 22 Realtime tests, Claude Fable 5.2 27 and 22, GPT-6 Astra 23 and 17, GPT-6.1 Sol 17 and 13, Claude Opus 5.5 10 and 4, and Gemini 4 Argon 15 and 5. When the clock keeps running, GPT-6.2 Astra no longer solves more tests than Fable: each solves 22 Realtime tests. Opus and Argon lose the most, and Realtime is the one setting where Argon solves more than Opus, by one test. Kimi 3.1 Pro solves 4 Realtime tests, as many as Opus; Gemini 3.8 Flash and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash solve 1 each, and GLM 5.3 Flash none.

The drop is not uniform, and some tasks go better in Realtime. Of the 54 tasks, 11 are solved in Standard by some agent but in Realtime by none, and 4 the other way round. Every strong agent except Argon solves some Realtime tests it fails in Standard: Fable 9, GPT-6.2 Astra 5, GPT-6 Astra 3, Sol 3 and Opus 2. The suites differ. In BiGym 2.0, 8 of 10 Realtime tests stay unsolved, against 2 of 10 in Standard, and in Embodied Olympics - Match 3 of 12 against 1 of 12. In RoboDynamicExam the direction reverses: every one of the 8 Realtime tests is solved by some agent, against 5 of 8 in Standard, and Fable and GPT-6.2 Astra each solve 6 Realtime tests there, against 3 and 5 Standard tests. In Realtime, 95% of the sampled attempts control the robot with a program, most of them one that runs the control loop (control approach), so tasks that reward a fast closed loop can gain from this setting.

The two-way comparisons between agents shift as well. In Realtime, 6 tests are solved only by Fable and 6 only by Astra, with 16 solved by both and 26 by neither. Sol solves 5 Realtime tests that Fable fails and 3 that Astra fails.

What access matters most? Privileged vs Standard

Three settings: what can agents observe and do?

The three tests of a task share the initial scene, the success check and the time limit, and a verifier outside the agent’s control grades every attempt. Every task must fail when the robot does nothing and pass with its reference solution wherever one exists, and the solutions of the original benchmarks are removed from the agent’s container. Only the agent’s access to the world changes (paper Figure 3, below).

In the Standard Setting, the agent runs in its own container and reaches the robot only through the robot’s interface: it requests camera images, depth and joint readings, submits chunks of actions, and must estimate poses from images and depth. It never gets object poses, segmentation, contact forces or the success check, and it cannot reset the scene unless the task grants a reset budget (seven suites do, with 3 to 50 resets). The simulator and the verifier share a second container and grade the live episode as it runs, so an error stays in the graded result. The simulation pauses while the agent thinks. The Realtime Setting is the same, except that the simulation never waits.

In the Privileged Setting, the agent shares a container with the simulator. It can read the exact state of every object (pose, size, velocity, joint positions), step the physics itself, call planning primitives, reset the scene without limit, and save and restore states. It submits a low-level trajectory (for some tasks, a controller program), which a separate verifier replays on a fresh copy of the scene, so mistakes made while exploring cost nothing. The time limit is the same as in Standard: Privileged gives more access, not more time. The tasks are designed so that this access is enough to find a solution by search; a high Privileged rate shows that a task is solvable, and the paper treats it as an approximate upper bound.

The tasks are also designed to stay hard under real-robot constraints, so a high Privileged rate with a low Standard rate is the intended result. The gap between them measures what an agent loses when it has to work like a real robot. The sections below measure how much Privileged success survives in Standard, and whether exact state or retries account for the gap.

Many tasks solved in Privileged fail in Standard

Between 38% and 60% of the tasks a strong agent solves in Privileged fail in Standard. On the 416 core tasks with both a Standard and a Privileged test, a large share of the tasks that each agent solves in Privileged fail in Standard: GPT-6.2 Astra fails 149 of 395 (38%), Claude Fable 5.2 140 of 337 (42%), GPT-6 Astra 199 of 333 (60%), GPT-6.1 Sol 183 of 309 (59%), Claude Opus 5.5 155 of 257 (60%) and Gemini 4 Argon 112 of 188 (60%). For the four weaker agents the share runs from 80% (Kimi 3.1 Pro, 132 of 165) to 91% (GLM 5.3 Flash, 40 of 44; Figure 11).

How much of the Privileged success carries over to Standard differs widely between agents. Of the core tasks it solves with privileged access, GPT-6.2 Astra also solves 62% in Standard (246 of 395), against 58% for Fable (197 of 337) and 40% for GPT-6 Astra (134 of 333), which runs in the same Codex harness. Standard takes away the exact state and the free retries together; the next sections separate the two.

The reverse is rare. The number of tasks solved in Standard but not in Privileged is 3 for GPT-6.2 Astra, 9 for Fable, 4 for GPT-6 Astra, 5 for Sol and 5 for Opus; Gemini 4 Argon has 20, the most of any agent.

The drop is broad. The paper reports that GPT-6.2 Astra’s success rate drops in all but one of the suites with at least five such tasks; that suite is MuJoCo Playground, where it solves all 7 tasks in both settings. Fable also drops in all but one, iSpatialGym, where it solves 7 of 8 tasks in both settings. GPT-6 Astra and Sol drop in every one of these suites. Opus does not drop in ManiGuard (7 of 7 in both) or DexToolBench (none in either). Argon shows a small reversal in MyoSuite (1 of 13 in Standard, 0 in Privileged) and ties in RoboLab (1 of 6 in both).

Agents that look even in Privileged can differ a lot in Standard. Of the 416 Privileged tests, only Fable solves 28, only GPT-6 Astra 24, both 309 and neither 55. In Standard, Fable keeps 197 of its Privileged successes and GPT-6 Astra 134 (Figure 11). What separates them is how much survives without exact state and free retries, the two features the next sections take apart.

Resets help more than exact object state

When the paper adds the Privileged features to the Standard interface one at a time, unlimited resets close more of the gap than exact object state. This suggests that the harder part for the agent is getting the action right, not knowing where things are.

The paper ran GPT-6 Astra in Codex with the reasoning effort (a setting that controls how long the model thinks before each response), time limit and tasks of its leaderboard run, changing only these features. The state query is read-only and returns, for every object, its position, orientation, size and bounding box, linear and angular velocity, and the joint positions of the robot and of articulated objects such as doors and drawers. It never returns success flags, scores, thresholds or goal regions, and it hides fields the agent is meant to find out itself, such as goal markers, hint notes and hazard labels. The reset feature raises the reset budget to 1,000 and adds calls that save the simulator state under a handle and restore it later. Runs are scored on the default test of each of the 391 core tasks with both settings (the Standard test, or the Realtime test for 50 of them). The 29% baseline is GPT-6 Astra’s leaderboard run on this set.

GPT-6 Astra solves 29% with the unmodified interface, 45% with the state query, 53% with unlimited resets and saved states, and 68% with both, still 11 points short of the 79% it reaches on the Privileged tests of the same tasks (Figure 12a). On the 50 Realtime tests, the gap between the two features is wider: 30% at baseline, 38% with the state query, 64% with resets, 80% with both and 84% in Privileged (paper Table 5).

Resets are used to retry hard stages, not to start over. Of the reset run’s 206 successes, only 14% came in the first episode, and 136 (66%) came in an episode that started from a saved mid-task state. The agent returns to just before a hard stage and tries it again without repeating the whole task. A real robot in the Standard Setting cannot do this: one slip at a hard stage usually ends the graded episode.

Exact state helps where cameras struggle. The paper finds the state query helps most where the scene is hard to measure from cameras, as in iSpatialGym and BEHAVIOR-1K, and changes little in Embodied Olympics (Fun) and DexHandExam. Perceiving the scene is a real problem in some suites. Across the whole set, however, the lack of exact state matters less than the lack of resets.

Limits. Neither added feature lets the agent step the physics or call planning primitives, as Privileged does, and the paper notes these abilities may account for part of the remaining 11 points.

Precise stages get much harder in Standard

Precise stages need a tight fit, a target pose or a moving contact; coarse stages only need the robot to reach a region, grasp, open or close. The stages that fail much more often in Standard than in Privileged are the ones that need precision. Among the stage types compared in at least two suites, the failure rate rises by 20 to 53 percentage points at precise types, but by only 3 to 17 points at seven of the nine coarse types (Figure 12b, above).

The paper splits stage types into two groups by their definitions. A stage is precise if it needs a tight fit, a mated or threaded relation, a target pose or path, or contact with a moving object; the 13 precise types are insert, assemble, screw or unscrew, thread or hang, connect, adjust pose, follow path, stabilize, regrasp, throw, strike, catch and dribble. A stage is coarse if it only needs to reach a region, a container, a grasp or the open or closed state of a mechanism; the 12 coarse types are place, put in, take out, gather, pick up, push, pull, open, close, press, switch on or off, and navigate. Material stages such as cleaning or pouring, and body stages other than navigate, are in neither group. A type’s failure rate in a setting is the number of its stages at which attempts fail, divided by the number that attempts reach, compared within each suite.

The precise types rise as follows (differences of the rounded rates in paper Table 13): assemble by 53 points, catch by 37, adjust pose by 36, screw or unscrew by 35, strike by 33, insert by 30, and thread or hang and follow path by 20 each. Among the coarse types, open and put in rise by 17, pick up by 14, place by 13, switch on or off and press by 4 and close by 3; the exceptions are take out (45 points) and navigate (38 points). Pooled within suites, the failure rate at precise stages is 43% in Standard against 14% in Privileged, across 12 suites, and at coarse stages 30% against 16%, across 25 suites. With suite fixed effects, precise stages rise by 15 points more than coarse ones (95% interval 9 to 21).

The paired check. Different attempts may reach different kinds of stages, so the paper also counts only the stages that the same agent reaches on the same task in both settings. There, the failure rate rises by 22.9 points at precise stages and 13.4 points at coarse stages, a smaller but still significant difference of 9.5 points (95% interval 4.7 to 14.7). Follow-path stages, most of them DexToolBench waypoints, make up 40% of these precise stages and rise by only 13 points; without them, precise stages rise by 29.7 points, 16.3 more than coarse ones (95% interval 10.3 to 22.7). Striking, assembling, adjusting a pose and catching rise by 30 to 46 points, while closing, placing and picking up rise by 2 to 15.

A precise stage that fails in Standard. In a DexHandExam task, a dexterous hand must seat the front guard of a fan on its rear guard and close four clips; the two upper clips need the guard to be seated. In Standard, Claude Opus 5.5 held the guard 36 to 39 degrees off the fan’s axis, where seating allows at most 5 degrees; pushing the guard on moved the whole fan, and the episode ended at 24 s (Figure 13). In Privileged, it closed the lower clips, seated the guard at 20 s and closed the upper clips. In the same Standard test, GPT-6 Astra seated the guard twice, each time for less than 2 s: as the hand withdrew its fingers, the guard came off with it and reopened a lower clip, and the attempt ended when the guard dropped. Six of the ten agents (GPT-6.2 Astra, Fable, GPT-6 Astra, Sol, Opus and Kimi 3.1 Pro) solve this task in Privileged; in Standard only GPT-6.2 Astra does.

The paper’s labels attribute the Opus failure to a wrong estimate of the guard’s pose in the hand. The labels show where the Standard Setting makes tasks harder; the controlled runs in the next section separate exact state from retries.

At precise stages, resets help more

Adding the state query rescues few failures at precise stages, which suggests that not seeing the exact pose is not the main cause. At precise stages, exact state solves 21% of GPT-6 Astra’s failures, about the same as a plain second try (19%), while unlimited resets solve 36%. At coarse stages the order flips: exact state solves 34% of the failures, against 14% for a second try.

The paper joins the controlled runs to GPT-6 Astra’s labeled failures on the default tests. Of these failures, 256 have a located failure stage: 91 at precise stages, 97 at coarse stages, 36 at material stages and 32 at other stages. Some failures would pass simply on a second attempt, so each run is compared with a second-try baseline: the run that adds neither feature and only hides the success signal (next section). It is not an exact repeat, since the agent must end the episode itself, but its overall success rate is within 4 points of the original run’s.

At precise stages, resets account for most of the gain. Resets solve 36% of the 91 failures at precise stages, almost twice the second try, and 31 of those 33 successes came only after a reset or a restored state. The difference from exact state is unlikely to be chance: of the 22 failures at precise stages that exactly one of the state run and the reset run solves, the reset run solves 18 (exact McNemar test, p = 0.004). Exact state does help once retries are available: with both features, the share rises from 36% to 47% (of 16 failures that only one of these two runs solves, the run with both solves 13, p = 0.021).

At coarse stages, exact state matters. Of the 97 coarse failures, exact state alone solves 34% and resets alone 44%. With both, the agent solves 67%, in line with the 62% that Privileged solves on the same tasks: for stages that only need a region, a container or a grasp, the two added features already reach roughly what full access to the simulator gives.

At precise stages, a gap to Privileged remains. With both features, the agent solves 47% of the failures at precise stages, against 74% in Privileged. On the failures at precise stages that have a Privileged test, the gain of the run with both over the second try is 52% of the gain that Privileged achieves (95% interval 34% to 69%). Privileged solves 26 failures at precise stages that the run with both does not, and misses 2 that it solves; 17 of the 26 are at follow-path, thread and assemble stages; at striking and catching stages, the two solve exactly the same failures. The remaining gap may come from stepping the physics one step at a time and calling planning primitives, which neither added feature provides; this suggests that precise stages benefit from testing a motion before running it in a graded attempt.

Limits. Failures at precise and coarse stages also come from different suites: most failures at precise stages are in Embodied Olympics (Fun), DexToolBench and RoboARC, while 38 of the 97 coarse failures are in BEHAVIOR-1K. The paper reports that leaving out any one suite reverses none of the comparisons, but its intervals and tests treat the failed tests as independent.

Does the agent need the information from the verifier?

In the Standard Setting, the verifier tells the agent the moment the task succeeds and ends the episode there; this is the success signal. Hiding it barely changes GPT-6 Astra’s success rate: on the default tests of the 391 core tasks with both settings, it solves 31% without the signal against 29% with it, and on the 50 Realtime tests, 26% against 30%. Exact state and, above all, the ability to retry matter far more.

In the no-signal run, no success flag, progress or score is returned, and the episode does not end when the success check passes. The agent has to judge that it is done and call finish, or the episode ends when time runs out or the task’s own failure rule triggers; the success check then runs on the final state. This is stricter in one way: a success that was reached and then undone, for example an object placed correctly and then knocked off, counts as a failure.

What this tells us. If agents relied on the signal to know when to stop, or to find a solution by trying moves until the flag appeared, hiding it should have cost many tests. The change, 2 points on the default tests and 4 on the Realtime tests, is within the run-to-run variation the paper reports (up to 5 points across three runs of GPT-6.1 Sol). When GPT-6 Astra reaches the goal, it usually knows it has. The information that limits it is not a verdict at the end of the episode but the ability to go back and retry a hard stage and, at coarse stages, an exact view of where things are.

This run also serves as a second try. Because hiding the signal changes so little, the paper uses this run as a rough stand-in for running each test again, the baseline in the precise and coarse comparison above.

At which stage do agents fail?

The verifier decides whether an attempt passed, but not where it went wrong. The paper therefore splits 427 of the 481 core tasks into stages in a task graph, and its progress maps show which stages hold at each moment of an attempt (Paper Figure 7). A failed attempt has at most one failure stage: the stage of an irreversible event, such as a fall, or else of the earliest mistake that the agent did not correct. The analyses in this part use the labeled attempts of nine agents.

Different agents often fail at the same stage

Each task is divided into stages, each a change in the world that the task requires: the fridge is open, the tomato is inside, the fridge is closed again. The failure analysis records the stage at which each failed attempt went wrong, so we can ask whether two agents that fail the same test fail at the same point. Often they do.

The paper measures this as the concentration of failure stages: the chance that two failed attempts on a test, picked at random, failed at the same stage (they always come from different agents, since each agent makes one attempt per test). On the Standard tests that at least two agents failed at located stages, on tasks with at least two stages, the concentration averages 57% over the nine labeled agents. If each failure fell at a random stage, with early stages favoured in the same proportion as on the other tasks, it would be 35% (Figure 14, panel (a)). In the Privileged Setting it is 49% against 31%.

The weaker agents do not drive this number, although they often fail before meeting any stage and two failures at the very first stage agree trivially. Among the five strong labeled agents alone, on the Standard tests where at least two of them failed at located stages, the concentration is 55%, against 30% for a random baseline built from their own failures on the other Standard tasks.

The agreement is also stronger than the kinds of stages alone predict. In a second baseline, each agent fails at each stage at the failure rate of that stage’s type on the other tasks, so a hard type of stage (wiping, say) attracts failures wherever it appears. This baseline gives 43% in Standard, still well below the observed 57%: particular stages of particular tasks are hard for many agents. Of the Standard tests with at least three stages and four or more located failures, 38 of 187 have at least 80% of their failures on a single stage. Examples are putting the first branch into the bin when cleaning up branches and twigs (BEHAVIOR-1K, 9 of 9 failures), putting the second spice jar onto a tray (Embodied Safety Exam, 8 of 8), and seating the front guard of a fan (DexHandExam, 8 of 8).

The shared stage is often the first one. Of the 255 Standard tests whose failures have a single most common stage, that stage is the task’s first stage in 63%, and in 53% of the tests whose tasks have at least two kinds of stages. Failing at the same stage does not mean failing in the same way: two agents that failed at the same Standard stage share a failure phenomenon (such as a lost hold or a collision) in only 39% of pairs.

Catching a ball twice (RoboDynamicExam) shows a shared stage that is not the first (Figure 15). A Franka arm holding a cup must catch a thrown ball, pour it into a slot, catch it again after it rolls down and rebounds off a ramp, and then pour it into a moving cart. In the Standard Setting all ten agents failed. Of the nine labeled agents, seven caught the first ball and then failed at the recatch, in different ways. In three attempts (GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 3.8 Flash) the cup touched the slot or the rebound ramp while pouring the ball out or moving downstream, which the task forbids and which ends the episode. GPT-6.1 Sol poured the ball into the slot by about 10 s, but at 16.7 s, as it adjusted the cup for the rebounding ball, the arm touched the scene. Claude Opus 5.5’s cup was still too high when the ball came off the ramp; Opus itself reported that the ball can be caught for only about 1.3 to 1.6 s after it leaves the cup, while straightening the cup after the pour alone takes about 1.4 s. In the runs of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM 5.3 Flash the ball never left the tilted cup. The two attempts that did not stop at the recatch went opposite ways: Kimi 3.1 Pro did not move the arm and failed at the first catch after about 1 s, while Claude Fable 5.2 recaught the ball at 13.6 s and got to the last stage, where the ball fell beside the moving cart at 18.2 s.

The other two settings suggest that the recatch is hard mainly because of timing and sensing under real-robot constraints, not because the agents misunderstand the task. In the Realtime Setting, the task’s default test, GPT-6.2 Astra, Claude Fable 5.2 and GPT-6.1 Sol passed, and all six located failures of the other labeled agents were again at the recatch. In the Privileged Setting, six agents passed: GPT-6.2 Astra, Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5 and Kimi 3.1 Pro, which had not moved the arm in Standard. Gemini 4 Argon and Gemini 3.8 Flash submitted no answer there, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM 5.3 Flash failed at the cart and at the recatch.

The failure stage often stays the same across settings

Agents solve far more tests in the Privileged Setting, yet the stage at which most failures of a task fall is usually the same in Standard and Privileged. The paper takes the 179 tasks whose failures have a single most common stage in both settings. In 134 of them, or 75%, that stage is the same, against 44% if the failure stages of each setting were drawn independently at random, with early stages favoured as before. Much of this agreement comes from tasks that simply fail at their first stage: in 85 of the 179 tasks, the common stage is the first stage in both settings. On the remaining 94 tasks the common stage is still the same in 52% (95% interval 42% to 63%), against 10% under the random baseline, not counting draws where both common stages are the first stage (Figure 14, panel (a), bottom bar).

Where the two common stages differ, the Standard one usually comes earlier in the task (in 35 tasks, against 10 where it comes later): under real-robot constraints, agents get stuck sooner.

The settings differ sharply after the hard stage. For each attempt, the paper takes the stage at which the other agents on the same test most often failed and asks whether the attempt got past it. Of the attempts that did, 42% passed the test in Standard, against 74% in Privileged; most of the other Standard attempts failed at a later stage. Over all attempts, the pass rates are 15% and 43%. Part of the link between getting past the stage and passing comes from stage order, since such an attempt is already closer to the end of the task. Even so, the gap between 42% and 74% suggests that in Standard, getting past the hardest stage is often not enough. Later stages that full access makes easy can still fail.

So the drop from Privileged to Standard does not come mainly from agents approaching the task differently and failing at new steps. The same steps are hard in both settings, and real-robot constraints make them harder and add failures after them. These counts describe where failures fall; they do not show that an agent follows the same plan in the two settings.

Material and body stages have the highest failure rates

Changing a material or moving the robot’s body fails most often, but which kinds of stage are hardest does not carry over from one suite to another. Every stage has a type naming the robot action that brings it about, and the types fall into categories: relation (placing or fitting one object relative to another), pose, mechanism (doors, lids, buttons), material (wiping, pouring, painting), body (walking, keeping balance), dynamic (catching, striking) and competition. A category’s failure rate is the share of its stages that attempts reach and then fail. Pooled over Standard and Privileged, the rates spread widely (Figure 14, panel (b)): material stages fail at 54% (649 of 1,211 reached), body stages at 36% (410 of 1,138), competition stages at 30%, relation stages at 27%, dynamic stages at 25%, pose stages at 18% and mechanism stages at 15% (329 of 2,231).

The extremes hold in each setting separately, and every category fails more often under real-robot constraints: material stages at 73% in Standard and 32% in Privileged, body stages at 55% and 21%, and mechanism stages at 20% and 10%. The pose, relation and dynamic categories lie between, in an order that differs between the settings, so the paper does not rank these three against each other.

Within a category, how the robot has to act matters as much as what it acts on. Among mechanism stages, opening a door or a lid fails at 28% (236 of 851), closing one at 12% when it is not already closed at the start, and pressing at 3% (13 of 432). Stages done by pressing or wiping fail at 6% when the robot presses on a mechanism such as a button, and at 58% when it wipes or presses on a material. Relation stages fail at 28% of reached stages with one hand or gripper, at 40% when two arms act together, and at 56% when the legs or torso carry the robot’s weight (paper Figure 29). Acting through a tool has mixed effects: stages on moving objects fail less often when the robot acts through a cup or a racket, while pose stages fail more often when it moves an object through a tool.

A difficulty ranking learned in some suites mostly does not predict difficulty in a new one. The paper holds out one suite at a time, ranks each pair of stage types by their failure rates on the other suites, and checks the ranking inside the held-out suite. It holds for 55% of 351 pairs of stage types, only slightly above the 50% expected by chance; for the mode of contact alone, 47% of 159 pairs; for the coarser stage categories, 63% of 79 pairs.

The agent is the strongest predictor of failure at a stage. The paper predicts whether an attempt that reaches a stage fails there from the setting plus one other attribute, with cross-validation over tasks, and reports how much each attribute improves on the setting alone: the agent by 13.5%, the stage type by 5.7%, the contact (who touches and how) by 5.7%, the stage category by 4.6%, the suite by 4.4% and the kind of robot body by 3.2%. So a specific stage of a specific task is often hard for many agents, but the general kind of that stage is a weak guide to difficulty in other suites.

Each suite has its own failure pattern

Broken down by suite, the 4,866 analyzed failures of the nine labeled agents show that the suite largely decides where and how agents fail (Figure 27).

The failure stage is often fixed by the suite’s design. Every RoboPaint failure is at a material stage, every Embodied Olympics - Match failure at a competition stage, and every DexToolBench failure with a located stage at a pose stage. In Embodied Olympics - Match and DexToolBench this follows from the tasks, since every stage of every task there is of that category. In other suites one category dominates without being the only one: dynamic stages hold 62% of RoboDynamicExam failures and 58% of Embodied Olympics - Fun failures, body stages 94% of MuJoCo Playground failures, and pose stages 75% of MyoSuite failures. Relation stages dominate the household and tabletop suites: 56% of BEHAVIOR-1K failures, 68% of RoboARC and BiGym 2.0, 88% of RoboDojo, 90% of KinDER, 91% of RoboCasa365 and all of RoboLab’s. Over all failures, relation stages are the most common, at 39%.

How far a failed attempt gets also differs sharply. In DexToolBench and KinDER, most failed attempts never meet a single stage of the task (65% and 75%), and in BiGym 2.0 49% meet none: the agent never establishes the first grasp or tool pose. In RoboPaint, 95% of failed attempts meet at least half of the stages, because painting attempts fail on how closely the strokes match the target, not on whether the robot can start. In Embodied Safety Exam and ManiGuard, some failed attempts meet every stage at one moment (14% and 25%) and still fail; in Embodied Safety Exam most of these break a safety constraint.

The same kind of stage can fail for different reasons in different suites. RoboLab and RoboCasa365 both fail mostly at relation stages, but RoboLab’s most common phenomenon is a lost hold (50%) and RoboCasa365’s a failure to make the contact the stage needs (36%). Losing balance or gait dominates where the robot carries its own weight: 86% of MuJoCo Playground failures, 48% of HumanoidBench and 34% of Embodied Olympics - Fun. It is also almost a third of BEHAVIOR-1K failures (30%), where a mobile manipulator can tip over while reaching or carrying. Embodied Safety Exam stands out for collisions (34%) and constraint violations (26%), which a safety suite is built to catch. In RoboDynamicExam, inaccurate poses (27%), collisions (22%) and timing errors (14%) all appear, matching the cup-and-ball failures above. DexToolBench’s most common phenomenon is a failure to make contact (41%).

Root causes point mostly to control, but support only broad conclusions. A control root cause appears in 69% of all failures and a cognitive one in 11%. Among suites with at least 20 failures, cognitive causes are most frequent in Embodied Safety Exam (37%), RoboPaint (32%) and iSpatialGym (28%), where the agent must read a rule, a target image or a spatial instruction correctly. In eleven suites with at least 20 failures, among them HumanoidBench, MyoSuite and DexToolBench, they stay under 8%.

Which robot bodies fail most?

Robots that carry their own weight often fall, and hands often fail to make contact. Figure 28 breaks the same 4,866 failures down by the kind of robot body, by agent and by setting. Each kind of body comes from only a few suites (three quarters of the mobile-manipulator failures are BEHAVIOR-1K’s, and all quadruped failures come from MuJoCo Playground).

Robots that support their own weight mostly fail by falling. Loss of balance or a gait failure is the most common phenomenon for quadrupeds (92% of their failures), humanoids (32%) and mobile manipulators (23%). Every quadruped failure is at a body stage. Humanoid failures are spread more widely, with 34% at dynamic stages and 28% at body stages, because many humanoid tasks come from the Embodied Olympics.

Hands often fail at the start of the task. Failure to make the contact a stage needs is the most common phenomenon for dexterous hands (30%) and for the musculoskeletal models of MyoSuite (33%). For dexterous hands, 53% of failed attempts never meet a stage of the task, and 69% of failures are at pose stages: the hand never reaches the grasp or tool pose that the rest of the task depends on.

Arms fail on accuracy and clearance. For robots with one or two arms, the most common phenomena apart from task-specific defects are inaccurate poses (20% for one arm, 25% for two) and collisions or obstructions (19% each). Two-armed robots fail at relation stages in 69% of cases, where both arms must place or thread an object together; one-armed robots fail at material stages in 37%, mostly painting.

The agents differ in how far they get and what goes wrong, not in which kind of stage stops them. For every labeled agent, relation stages hold the largest share of failures, from 37% (GLM 5.3 Flash) to 43% (GPT-6.1 Sol), and the other categories differ by about six points or less between agents. The weaker agents’ failures more often meet no stage of the task (26% to 37%, against 15% to 23% for the five strong labeled agents) and more often lack the needed contact (per contact stage reached, see Weaker agents). A lost hold makes up a larger share of the failures of Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol (16% to 17% across all three settings) than of any weaker agent (9% to 12%), partly because an agent that reaches more grasp stages has more chances to lose a hold: per grasp stage reached, GPT-6 Astra loses the hold in 5.4% of cases against 6.7% for the other eight labeled agents pooled (the interval includes zero). Among the strong agents, the share of failures whose progress could not be read ranges from 5% (Claude Fable 5.2) and 6% (GPT-6.1 Sol) to 10% (Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 4 Argon) and 11% (GPT-6 Astra), and Gemini 4 Argon’s failures more often carry the not-attempted label (15%, the highest of the nine labeled agents). Kimi 3.1 Pro (49%) and Gemini 4 Argon (42%) have many failures with no root cause.

The settings change the kind of failure in ways that match what each setting lets the agent observe and do. Realtime failures more often lie at competition stages (22%, against 2% in Standard and in Privileged) and dynamic stages (24%, against 9% and 7%), and more often involve a timing error (15%, against 2% and 1%), because in Realtime the world keeps moving while the agent thinks. Collisions are more common in Standard failures (14%, against 5% in Realtime and 7% in Privileged), losses of balance are more common in Standard (17%) and Realtime (18%) than in Privileged (10%), and Privileged failures more often carry the not-attempted label (14%, against 3% in Standard and 7% in Realtime).

Weaker agents often fail before contact, strong agents usually after

The weaker agents do not fail the same steps more often than the strong ones; they fail earlier. Their attempts more often end before meeting any stage of the task, and at stages that need contact they miss the contact several times more often. Almost everything a weaker agent solves, some strong agent also solves.

Many weaker-agent attempts meet no stage at all. Among failed Standard attempts on tasks with at least two stages, panel (a) counts those that met no stage: 44% (Kimi 3.1 Pro, 117 of 264) to 59% (DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM 5.3 Flash) for the four labeled weaker agents, against 26% to 36% for the five strong labeled agents. In these attempts the robot never opened the cabinet, never picked up the first item, or fell over while looking around, as in the cases below.

Once at a contact stage, weaker agents miss the contact several times more often. Failing to make the required contact is 16.5% to 24.2% of the weaker agents’ failed attempts, against 5.7% to 12.5% for the strong agents. A lost hold looks rarer for the weaker agents mainly because they get fewer chances. A lost hold makes up 9.4% to 12.5% of the weaker agents’ failed attempts, less than for most strong agents. Per grasp stage reached, the order reverses: the weaker agents lose the hold at 8.1% to 9.8% of grasp stages, the strong agents at 4.0% to 7.0%.

Which control approaches do agents use?

Control directly, write programs, or train a policy?

An agent can control the robot in three ways, or mix them. In direct control, the model itself is the controller: it sends a move, looks at the camera images or sensor readings, and chooses the next move. In code to control, it writes a planner, a controller or another program, and the program produces the robot’s actions. In train a policy, it trains a policy in simulation and lets the policy control the robot. The figure shows one attempt of each kind, with an excerpt of what the agent wrote.

In a random sample of attempts by the nine labeled agents, agents controlled the robot directly in 61% of the Standard attempts and wrote programs to control it in 95% of the Realtime attempts and 83% of the Privileged attempts. A likely reason is how the simulation handles time. In Standard the simulation waits while the model thinks, so the model can sit inside the control loop and choose each move after it looks. In Realtime the simulation keeps running, so a program has to run the loop. In Privileged the agent has the full simulator state and can compute or search for a motion before it submits it.

What the sample covers. A separate coding agent read the logs of the 2,359 attempts and labeled each with the approach that produced its graded answer. The attempts were sampled at random from the runs of every agent except GPT-6.2 Astra. The authors then checked and finalized every label.

Standard: the model is the controller. Of the 1,137 Standard attempts, 61% used direct control: the model sends one chunk of actions, looks at the camera images or sensor readings, and chooses the next chunk while the robot waits. Only 32% wrote a program to control the robot (9% open-loop, 15% closed-loop, 8% parameter search). Another 6% mixed approaches and 1% had no control plan.

Realtime: a program runs the loop. Of the 161 Realtime attempts, 95% used a program and none used direct control. Most programs are closed-loop, reading the robot’s state or cameras while they run: 75% of all Realtime attempts, against 16% open-loop programs and 4% parameter searches. In the paper’s badminton example from Embodied Olympics - Match, a program written by GPT-6 Astra ran in the background, located the shuttlecock with two cameras, fitted a drag model to its flight and swung the racket. Whenever play paused, the agent read the program’s logs and revised it, and it won 11 to 4 against an opponent that picks its shots with a learned policy.

Privileged: compute or search first, then submit. Of the 1,061 Privileged attempts, 83% used a program: 18% open-loop, 45% closed-loop and 20% parameter search. The parameter searches, 216 of the 876 programs, try many episodes in the simulator until a motion passes. Direct control falls to 10%, and 5% of the attempts had no control plan.

The same agent changes approach when the setting changes. On a RoboTwin 2.0 task that asks for three bottles to go into a dustbin, GPT-6 Astra in the Standard Setting sent end-effector commands one at a time and checked the camera images after each. It knocked one bottle over, picked it up again with the other arm, and finished about 30 seconds before the time limit. In the Privileged Setting it read the task’s source code, scripted the grasps with the simulator’s motion primitives, handed the bottles from one arm to the other in mid-air and submitted after five minutes.

Training a policy is rare unless the test supplies the tools. Only 13 of the 2,359 sampled attempts trained a policy: 0.6% of the Standard sample, 2% of the Realtime sample and 0.2% of the Privileged sample. Many Privileged tests give the agent a GPU to run the simulator, but their instructions name no training library. Apart from these, only eight tests give the agent a GPU: the GPU versions of a soccer match and of a table tennis match in each setting, and two RoboEngineer tests that ask the agent to train a walking policy. When a test does supply a GPU and a training library, agents train, as the soccer match below shows.

How do solved runs succeed? Learn from failure

Four methods recur in the solved runs, and all of them concern measuring the physical world rather than the code itself. The agent models the scene from depth: it turns depth images into point clouds and fits the objects it must handle, such as the rings of a rope course or the cells of a bookcase. It builds a local model of the robot, from the robot’s public model or from its own fits of how the robot moves, and tunes its motions there before trying them on the robot. It plans offline and executes in long stretches, sending a motion it has already checked instead of adjusting the motion step by step. It treats a failed episode as a measurement: where a test allows several episodes, it uses a failure to learn what it could not see, such as a latch that needs a deep press or the flight of a tossed ball.

Other agents use the same methods on tests they solved, according to the paper’s run descriptions. Gemini 4 Argon located a table and a white mug in the point clouds of three calibrated depth cameras in RoboLab, found that the 9 cm mug was wider than the gripper’s largest opening, and grasped the mug’s wall at the rim with one finger inside it. Claude Opus 5.5 estimated a glass jar’s shape and the position of its hinge from the depth point cloud in ManiGuard, swung the lid shut about the hinge and lifted the jar by 1 cm to check the grasp before carrying it to the goal; GPT-6 Astra knocked the same jar over.

Safety tests reward the same habit of measuring. In the Embodied Safety Exam, the agents that passed three Standard tests solved by at most three of the ten agents measured before they acted. Claude Opus 5.5 switched off a live power strip by lowering the gripper 4 mm at a time until the force reading jumped. Gemini 4 Argon found that the straight path to the tray crossed a lit candle, rejected an arc that came within 0.157 m of it, and took one that kept at least 0.39 m away. Gemini 3.8 Flash measured a 0.51 m gap for its 0.482 m base and reported the task infeasible, which was the correct answer.

The methods do not guarantee that a grasp or contact will hold; they also appear in failed runs. GPT-6 Astra built a local model on the catch-and-shoot test below and ran out of time. Does spending more help?

Failed attempts cost more

Across agents, the cost of an attempt and the chance of success are only loosely related. Within one agent, the expensive attempts are mostly failures. A failing agent often keeps working, so its cost keeps rising. For the six strong agents, the Privileged Setting raises success sharply while lowering the cost per attempt. Cost mostly reflects how long the agent worked, and a higher cost does not by itself bring success.

How cost is measured. The paper prices each attempt from the token counts of its API requests at the providers’ list prices (Figure 21), not at what anyone was billed. The 9,440 attempts with a recorded cost across the ten agents and all settings (of 9,510 counted attempts) add up to about $69,100.

For every agent, failure is the expensive outcome. In both Standard and Privileged, the median failed attempt costs more than the median solved one, for all ten agents. Standard medians, solved against failed, are $2.76 and $5.75 for GPT-6.2 Astra, $7.88 and $12.76 for Fable, $7.55 and $25.59 for GPT-6 Astra, $1.01 and $3.06 for Sol, $2.96 and $4.62 for Opus, and $2.68 and $5.55 for Argon. Costlier suites do not explain this: within the same suite, a failed Standard attempt typically costs 1.28 times (GLM 5.3 Flash) to 1.92 times (Gemini 3.8 Flash) as much as a solved one.

For some agents, much of the extra cost comes from extra time. 51.3% of GPT-6 Astra’s failed Standard attempts, 53.1% of Sol’s and 78.0% of Argon’s ran to at least 95% of the time limit, against 2.3%, 3.7% and 8.7% of their solved attempts. For GPT-6.2 Astra the two shares are 36.6% and 1.4%. Only 8.8% of Fable’s and 6.4% of Opus’s failed Standard attempts ran that long, yet their failures also cost more than their successes.

Information matters more than spending. For the six strong agents, a Privileged attempt costs less on average than a Standard one and succeeds far more often: GPT-6.2 Astra $6.55 against $7.24, Fable $11.01 against $13.05, GPT-6 Astra $22.28 against $26.04, Sol $3.82 against $3.94, Opus $4.64 against $6.42, and Argon $4.14 against $6.08. What lifts success there is what the agent can know and undo (exact object state, unlimited resets with saved states, a private simulator), not what it spends.

Cost and success are only loosely tied

Similar success at very different prices. GPT-6.1 Sol costs $3.9 per Standard attempt at list prices, about a seventh of GPT-6 Astra’s $26.0, and solves 163 of the 481 Standard tests against GPT-6 Astra’s 175. It also solves more than Claude Opus 5.5 (130) and Gemini 4 Argon (127), whose attempts cost about a quarter of GPT-6 Astra’s.

A high price does not guarantee success. Kimi 3.1 Pro and Gemini 3.8 Flash spend more per attempt than Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon, yet solve less than half as many Standard tests, 55 and 32. The agent that solves the most Standard tests, GPT-6.2 Astra (279), is not the most expensive one: its attempts cost $7.2 on average.

Why price and success come apart. The cost of an attempt mostly reflects two things: the model’s price per token and how long the agent keeps working. Neither measures how well the agent plans. As shown above, a long attempt is more often a failed one, and raising GPT-6.1 Sol’s reasoning effort barely changes its success rate or its cost (see Reasoning effort: does thinking longer help the same model?). A higher bill per attempt is a weak sign of a better agent.

Across the ten agents, the more expensive ones do tend to solve more, but the spread around that trend is wide, as the examples above show.

How sensitive are the results to the harness, task instruction, reasoning effort and repeated runs?

Harness: the same model, up to 7.5 points apart

Every agent in Embodied First Exam is a model running inside a harness: the program that hands the model its tools, keeps its conversation, and decides when a session ends. Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Opus 5.5 and two other models (Kimi 3.1 Pro and GLM 5.3 Flash) run in Claude Code, GPT-6.2 Astra, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex, the two Gemini models in Antigravity CLI, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash in DeepSeek Harness, DeepSeek’s own. To measure the harness’s share, the paper ran three models in other harnesses on the 456 core Standard tests, with the leaderboard’s time limits and reasoning effort (Figure 16c). The effect ranges from 7.5 points to almost nothing, and a model’s own harness is not always its best.

GPT-6 Astra loses 7.5 points outside Codex. It solves 157 of 456 tests (34.4%) in Codex, its own harness, and 123 (27.0%) in Claude Code. Single tests move both ways: 75 are solved only in Codex and 41 only in Claude Code. The paper finds the difference significant (p = 0.002). Most of it is already there in the first episode, before any reset: 119 successes in Codex against 95 (p = 0.015). So the gap does not come only from how the harnesses handle long sessions with many retries.

Claude Opus 5.5 is about the same in both. It solves 119 of 456 in Codex and 114 in its own Claude Code, and 97 against 96 in the first episode. Underneath, 35 tests are solved only in Codex and 30 only in Claude Code. Neither count differs significantly.

Gemini 4 Argon does best in another harness. It solves 132 of 456 in Codex, 116 in Claude Code and 110 in its own Antigravity CLI. Against its own harness, Codex wins 47 tests and loses 25, a net 22 (p = 0.013), and BEHAVIOR-1K contributes 10 of the 22, the most of any suite. Claude Code and Antigravity CLI are close (p = 0.61). The first-episode order is the same, 114, 109 and 96, and the Codex advantage holds there too (p = 0.036).

How the cross-provider runs work. Codex and Claude Code speak only their own provider’s API format. In the cross-provider runs, an adapter passes each request to the other provider, translates the answer back, and adapts the system prompt and model metadata. The harness runs are reported separately and do not change the leaderboard.

So when one agent solves a test that another fails, part of the difference can come from the harness. The paper therefore reports every result for a model in a particular harness: how the harness exposes tools, manages the context and ends a session is part of what Embodied First Exam measures.

Hang Mugs: which arms count

RoboDojo’s prompt reads “Hang all the mugs on the mug rack”. The rack has six arms at three heights. A mug counts as hung only when its handle lines up within 60° with the lower arm pointing right, so the four arms that point left or right count, while the two middle arms are 80° and 82° off and do not. Nothing in the scene shows this, so an agent that is not told must guess which arms count. The fully specified prompt states it: “A mug hanging through its handle on one of the four arms that point left or right passes; on the middle arms that point toward or away from you its cross direction is about 80 deg off and it fails”.

A fair concern is whether spelling out the finished state amounts to handing the agent the grading rubric. In part, it does: the agent learns what counts as done, and the verification code itself stays hidden. But the agent still has to perceive the scene, plan, and act to get there, and that is what matters most: turning a coding agent’s skills into manipulation in the physical world is the main bottleneck, and the fully specified prompt does not remove it. Following Terminal-Bench’s task guidelines, the description “completely describes what the verification algorithm will look for”, and, as its review criteria put it, states “what to achieve, not how”. One reading is that spelling out the finished state makes the task easier than it should be, since inferring what done means is part of the challenge. Another is that without it the task is under-defined, since some requirements, such as which arms of the rack count, cannot be seen in the scene, and a person given the same task would likely ask. Both readings are reasonable, and the line is least clear for exact thresholds such as 60° or a lid 90% closed. Where a clear task description ends and a hint begins is an open question, and the score gap shows how much that line matters.

Reasoning effort: does thinking longer help the same model?

Another way to spend more is to let the model think longer before each action. Every leaderboard agent runs at high reasoning effort; the paper also ran GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex on the 456 core Standard tests at medium and at maximum effort, with everything else as in the leaderboard run.

The three efforts are within run noise of each other. Medium and maximum effort solve 30.7% and 30.9% in one run each. The three high-effort runs solve 32.2%, 32.2% and 27.9% of the same tests (mean 30.8%, standard deviation 2.5 points; see run variance), and both single runs fall inside that range. We tested one model, with one run at medium and one at maximum effort, so differences of a few points would not show.

What changes is the way the agent works. With more effort, Sol writes more output tokens, sends fewer requests and runs longer: it thinks more before each step and takes fewer, slower steps. Both styles solve about the same share of these physical tasks.

The cost stays about the same, $3.91 to $4.10 per attempt, probably because fewer requests offset the extra output tokens.

Repeated runs: Sol’s success rate varies by less than 5 points

The leaderboard counts one attempt per agent and test. To measure how much the success rate changes from one run to the next, the paper ran GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex on the 456 core Standard tests two more times at high effort, with everything else as in the leaderboard run.

The three runs’ success rates are within five percentage points of each other, with a mean of 30.8% and a standard deviation of 2.5 points, and each run by itself would rank fourth in the Standard column of the leaderboard, behind GPT-6.2 Astra, Claude Fable 5.2 and GPT-6 Astra and ahead of Claude Opus 5.5.

Reasoning effort moves the result less than repeating the run: Sol at medium and maximum effort solves 30.7% and 30.9% of the Standard tests, inside the range of the three high-effort runs (details).

Embodied Olympics: Can agents do continual learning?

In Embodied Olympics, agents build and run the systems that control robots in real-time matches against each other, on two boards. On Continual Learning, each agent keeps one workspace for the whole championship, so its programs, policy weights and notes carry from match to match. On Episodic Learning, every match starts from an empty workspace.

Agents rarely turn what they see into a better program. In 75% of the 515 Episodic agent-match pairs, the agent notices how the opponent plays, and in 66% it analyzes the opponent correctly. Far fewer act on it: only 26% change their program, and only 4% make a change that wins more points. The side with the better first program wins 89 of 98 decisive matches, and three of six agents improve over a Continual season.

For the full analysis, see the Embodied Olympics report.

Limitations and Next Steps on Hidden Tasks and Real Robots

Every comparison here comes from one attempt per agent and test; three runs of GPT-6.1 Sol in the Standard Setting give a success rate of 30.8% ± 2.5 (see Repeated runs: Sol’s success rate varies by less than 5 points). The stage labels behind At which stage do agents fail? come from a labeling pipeline that combines labeling by coding agents (GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Sonnet 5.5) with human spot checks while the prompts were developed. All results except one real-robot demonstration are in simulation. The paper discusses these and further limits, including possible memorization of public benchmarks and how the core set was screened (paper, Limitations).

Because results on tasks adapted from public benchmarks may partly reflect what a model has seen before, we are building a hidden set of held-out tasks that will not be released. Agents will be evaluated on it under the same settings and the same external verifier, so a result there shows how an agent handles a task it could not have seen.

The Privileged Setting also lets an agent search for an oracle solution, so the exam can provide training data as well as a test; we are exploring this use.

The Standard Setting gives the agent only the robot’s sensor readings and control interface, so the same interface can drive a physical robot once the outcome can be graded. We are extending Embodied First Exam to real robots, with tasks and protocols that let a real-robot attempt be run and graded consistently.

As a first test on hardware, the authors connected GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 to a physical robot arm playing a buzz wire game (paper Figure 35). The robot holds the handle of a metal loop and must guide it along a bent wire fixed to a wooden board without touching it. Loop and wire form one electric circuit, so any contact turns on a light and a buzzer and ends the trial, as in RoboARC’s simulated buzz wire tasks on a Franka Panda arm. The agent sends discrete commands: move the gripper up, down, left or right, or rotate it either way. Each agent ran eight trials in a row on the same wire, each scored by progress, the share of the wire the loop covered before the trial ended (100% is the whole wire without a contact).

Both agents scored higher in the last four trials than in the first four: average progress rose from about 65% to about 94% for GPT-6 Astra and from about 35% to about 63% for Opus. GPT-6 Astra started at about 60%, dipped once (from about 80% in trial 3 to about 60% in trial 4), and covered the whole wire in trials 7 and 8. Opus scored 0% in trial 1, then varied between about 40% and 80% (about 80% in trial 6, 40% in trial 7, 80% in trial 8) and never covered the whole wire. GPT-6 Astra scored higher in every trial and reached at least 80% in five of its eight trials, Opus in two.

This demonstration shows that a coding agent can control a physical robot arm in this game.
