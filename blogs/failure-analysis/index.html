Fable 5.2 on EFE: Where Today’s Agents Succeed and Fail in Robotics

November 2026 · EFE Blog · Leaderboard · Paper · Code

English中文Español

Research question

What robotic tasks can general-purpose coding agents solve, under what physical-world constraints, and where do they fail?

The first report describes the exam; this post asks what its results say about coding agents. What sets the exam apart is that it controls exactly what an agent can see and do. Each task runs in three settings with the same scene, success rule and time limit: in the Standard Setting the agent sees only the robot’s sensors and cannot reset, as with a real robot; the Realtime Setting is the same, but the world keeps running while the agent thinks; the Privileged Setting gives the full simulator state and unlimited resets. Comparing them shows which information and abilities an agent depends on. Ten agents each made one attempt per test on the 456 hard core tasks.

What we found

Outline

  1. Which tasks does each agent fail, and why?
    1. Each strong agent solves tests the others cannot, in both directions
    2. Even with ten agents pooled, more than a third of the Standard tests stay unsolved
    3. The lead changes hands from suite to suite, and a newer agent can do worse on specific suites
    4. Some suites are mostly out of reach for every agent under real-robot rules, but almost none with full simulator access
    5. Every agent finds carrying objects easiest and using tools hardest, while the middle types change order
    6. Astra keeps checking and correcting until time runs out; Fable plans ahead and stops once it expects to miss
    7. Fable’s misses on widely solved tests come from an unwanted contact, a skipped check, or no margin left
    8. Three runs of the same agent give success rates within five points of each other
    9. When the simulation keeps running, the boundary moves further in, though not on every task
  2. What information matters to the agent? Privileged versus Standard
    1. The three settings differ only in what the agent can see and do
    2. Full simulator access hides much of what makes a task hard
    3. Being able to retry helps more than knowing where everything is
    4. The drop concentrates at stages that need a tight fit, a target pose or a moving contact
    5. At precise stages, seeing the exact pose barely helps on its own; retrying the action does
    6. GPT-6 Astra barely depends on being told that the task has succeeded
  3. Where in a task do agents fail?
    1. When several agents fail a task, they tend to fail at the same stage, so the hard step belongs to the task more than to the agent
    2. Full simulator access changes what happens at and after the hard stage, not where the hard stage is
    3. Changing a material or moving the robot’s body fails most often, but which kinds of stage are hardest does not carry over from one suite to another
    4. Each suite fails in its own way, because the suite fixes which kinds of stages, bodies and dangers an agent meets
    5. The robot’s body shapes what goes wrong: robots that carry their own weight fall, and hands fail to make contact
    6. Weaker agents fail earlier: often before any progress or before touching the object
    7. Fable rarely fails before touching the object; when it fails, it is usually after contact, with a misjudged pose or a lost grip
    8. On long household tasks, many agents complete the first items and then lose the robot’s balance on the way to the next
    9. Fetching cereal and a bowl: the strong agents fail with the bowl in reach, the weaker ones before getting hold of what their stage needs
  4. Which control approach do agents use?
    1. The setting decides how agents act: the model drives the robot in Standard, and programs drive it in Realtime and Privileged
    2. Given a GPU and a training library, agents switch from hand-written gaits to trained policies, and in Standard and Realtime almost every win comes from a policy they trained
  5. What successful trajectories look like
    1. Solved runs measure before they move: they model the scene and the robot, rehearse offline, and treat a failed episode as data
  6. Does spending more help?
    1. Spending more is not what buys success: failed attempts cost more than solved ones, and an agent at less than a sixth of the price comes within about two points
    2. The agents spend an attempt in different ways: Fable writes more and acts less, Astra acts more and runs to the time limit, Opus stops earliest
    3. Thinking harder changes how GPT-6.1 Sol works but hardly changes how often it succeeds
  7. How the harness affects results
    1. An agent’s score belongs to the model and its harness together: the same model moves by up to 7.5 points when the harness changes
    2. How a harness ends a session and summarizes a long one can decide whether a physical task gets finished
  8. Does the task instruction change the result?
    1. A case from RoboDojo: stating the success check in full triples the score
  9. What’s next
    1. Limitations
    2. A hidden set of tasks, so that results measure ability rather than memory
    3. Evaluation on real robots, starting from the same Standard interface
    4. More runs per test, and more tasks where the clock keeps running
    5. A separate board, with its own rules: the Embodied Olympics Episodic Learning matches
    6. Where each number comes from
    7. Data and citation

Overall results

Where Fable leads

  • BEHAVIOR-1K, Standard: 26 of 71 long household activities, against 14 for Astra, 13 for Sol, 5 for Opus and 4 for Argon.
  • RoboARC, Standard: 32 of 50 puzzles, against at most 15 for any other agent.
  • iSpatialGym (7 of 8) and Embodied Olympics - Match (10 of 13), where the next agents solve 5 and 6.

Where Astra and Sol lead

  • RoboPaint, Standard: GPT-6 Astra solves 7 of 22 tests and GPT-6.1 Sol 6, against 2 each for Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon.
  • Astra is ahead in Standard DexToolBench (2 of 20), HumanoidBench (1 of 8, the only success by any agent in the suite) and MuJoCo Playground (4 of 7), and in Privileged DexToolBench (13 of 20, against 10 for Fable and Sol).
  • In Privileged, Sol solves the most MyoSuite tests (7 of 13) and BEHAVIOR-1K tests (38 of 71).

What others solve that Fable cannot, and the reverse

  • Of the 230 Standard tests Fable fails, Astra solves 39, Sol 29, Opus 22 and Argon 14; together the four solve 60. Fable solves 108 Standard tests that Astra fails.
  • Sol solves 49 Standard tests that Astra fails, Opus 46 and Argon 34. Opus solves all 7 ManiGuard tests, which no other agent does.
  • In Privileged the two directions are nearly even: 28 tests only Fable solves and 24 only Astra solves, out of 391.
  • 169 of the 230 Standard tests Fable fails are solved by no agent at all.

Which tasks does each agent fail, and why?

Each strong agent solves tests the others cannot, in both directions

No strong agent’s solved tests are a subset of another’s: in the Standard Setting this holds for every pair of the five strong agents, in both directions, as the paper states. Figure 9 shows it test by test (each column is one test, each row one agent, and a filled cell is a solved attempt, shaded darker the more its attempt cost at list price). Whichever agent the tests are sorted by, other agents still have filled cells to the right of its last success.

Claude Fable 5.2 fails 230 of the 456 Standard tests. GPT-6 Astra solves 39 of them, GPT-6.1 Sol 29, Claude Opus 5.5 22 and Gemini 4 Argon 14; since these sets overlap, the four together solve 60. The weaker agents solve a few (Kimi 3.1 Pro 7, Gemini 3.8 Flash 6, Qwen3.8 Max 3, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 3, GLM 5.3 Flash 0), and all but one of these are also solved by a strong agent; the exception is a RoboDojo block-swapping test that only Kimi solves. Of the tests Fable fails, 31 have exactly one other solver: Astra for 17, Sol for 8, Opus for 4, and Argon and Kimi for 1 each.

The reverse direction is larger. Fable solves 108 Standard tests that Astra fails, against the 39 Astra solves that Fable fails; both solve 118 and neither solves 191. Against Sol the split is 108 and 29, and against Opus 134 and 22. Still, each of those 39, 29 and 22 tests is one where the agent with fewer successes did something the other did not.

Of the Standard tests Astra fails, Sol solves 49, Opus 46, Argon 34 and Kimi 13. For several pairs the two directions are close: Sol solves 49 tests Astra fails and Astra 59 that Sol fails, and Argon solves 47 that Opus fails against Opus’s 51 that Argon fails. Opus and Astra are further apart, 46 against 89. The table gives every pair.

Tests that only one strong agent solves are spread across agents too. Of the 286 Standard tests that any strong agent solves, 79 have exactly one strong solver: 48 Fable, 18 Astra, 8 Sol, 4 Opus and 1 Argon. They fall in different suites: 25 of Fable’s 48 are in BEHAVIOR-1K or RoboARC, while 4 of Astra’s 18 and 2 of Sol’s 8 are RoboPaint tests.

A failure by the agent with the most successes does not mark a test as hard. Eight tests Fable fails are solved by at least four of the other nine agents, and one, an Embodied Safety Exam pour, by six, including Kimi, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Qwen and DeepSeek (all eight are listed in Fable’s misses on widely solved tests).

With full simulator access the split between Fable and Astra is nearly even: in Privileged, 28 tests only Fable solves and 24 only Astra solves, with 284 solved by both and 55 by neither (391 tests). Astra is the only strong solver of 12 Privileged tests, Fable of 9, Sol of 5, Argon of 2 and Opus of 1. The Realtime split is in the Realtime section.

Even with ten agents pooled, more than a third of the Standard tests stay unsolved

Pooling all ten agents, 287 of the 456 Standard tests are solved by at least one and 169 (37%) by none. The five strong agents together solve 286 (63%), and the five weaker agents add one test. This shared boundary is the clearest measure in the exam of what current coding agents cannot yet do under real-robot rules, and it does not depend on which agent we look at.

Most of what one agent fails, every agent fails. Of the 230 Standard tests Claude Fable 5.2 fails, 169 are these unsolved tests; only about a quarter (61) are tests another agent solved.

In Standard, the five strong agents solve 226, 157, 147, 114 and 110 of 456 tests, and the five weaker agents 46, 29, 17, 14 and 11. Few tests are solved by many agents: 33 by all five strong agents, and only one, a button-press test, by all ten. At the other end, 77 tests are solved by exactly one agent.

The unsolved tests cluster in a few suites: BEHAVIOR-1K contributes 36 of the 169, Embodied Olympics - Fun 26, DexToolBench 17, RoboARC 14 plus 7 IKEA assembly tests, RoboPaint 11, MyoSuite 8 and HumanoidBench 7 (suites out of reach looks at these suites).

This gap is what the exam is designed to produce: tasks whose Privileged tests can be solved by search, but that stay hard under real-robot rules. Of the 169 Standard tests no agent solves, 160 belong to tasks that also have a Privileged test, and some agent solves 122 of those Privileged tests. Only 38 stay unsolved in both settings; the other 9 have no Privileged test. Across the whole Privileged Setting, only 43 of 391 tests (11%) are solved by no agent, 19 of them in BEHAVIOR-1K. The strong agents together solve 348 Privileged tests (89%), and the weaker agents add none.

The counts do not say why a test is unsolved. The paper’s stage-level failure labels (a stage is one change in the world that the task requires, such as the fridge being open; coding agents labeled each failed attempt from its replay and logs, and we spot-checked the labels by hand while developing the prompts) say at which stage an attempt fails and what went wrong there, but they cannot separate a perception error from a control error, and their root-cause layer supports few firm conclusions.

The lead changes hands from suite to suite, and a newer agent can do worse on specific suites

Broken down by suite, the agent with the most successes changes several times. The counts are from the site data of the paper’s core set and use the paper’s suite split, with the IKEA assembly tasks of RoboARC counted as their own suite.

Where GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol lead. RoboPaint is the clearest case: in Standard, Astra solves 7 of 22 painting and calligraphy tests and Sol 6, against 2 each for Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon. Astra also has the most Standard successes in DexToolBench (2 of 20), HumanoidBench (1 of 8, the only success by any strong agent in the suite) and MuJoCo Playground (4 of 7). The one HumanoidBench success is the cube manipulation, where a humanoid rotates a cube above each palm; Fable fails it in Standard but solves the same task in both Realtime and Privileged. With full simulator access Astra still leads DexToolBench (13 of 20, against 10 each for Fable and Sol) and RoboPaint (22 of 22, against 20 each for Fable and Sol), while Sol leads MyoSuite (7 of 13, against 6 each for Fable and Astra) and BEHAVIOR-1K (38 of 71, against 36 each for Fable and Astra).

In our reading of the runs, the RoboPaint gap in Standard comes from how the agents judge their copy through the cameras rather than from painting; Check vs stop shows the runs.

Where Claude Fable 5.2 leads. Its largest margins are in long, many-step suites. In BEHAVIOR-1K, the long household activities, it solves 26 of 71 Standard tests, against 14 for Astra and 13 for Sol. In RoboARC, the fine-manipulation puzzles, it solves 32 of 50, while each of the other strong agents solves 11 to 15. It also leads in iSpatialGym (7 of 8) and Embodied Olympics - Match (10 of 13). The others can do these suites: with full simulator access, Astra and Sol solve about as many BEHAVIOR-1K tests as Fable (36 and 38 of 71, against 36), so Fable’s Standard margin there appears tied to working under real-robot rules.

Where Claude Opus 5.5 leads. Opus solves all 7 ManiGuard tests in Standard, the only agent of the ten to do so. ManiGuard states its safety rules in the instruction, and it is the one Standard suite where the five strong agents average at least 70%. Opus also has the most Standard successes in RoboWits (4 of 6). With 114 Standard tests overall, fewer than Fable, Astra or Sol, Opus shows that a lower total can coexist with the best result on a whole suite.

A newer agent can drop on specific suites. GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra run in the same harness, Codex, and Sol is the newer model. Sol solves 147 Standard tests, 10 fewer than Astra’s 157, and the drop is concentrated: in BiGym 2.0 Astra solves 4 of 10 Standard tests and Sol none, in RoboDojo 3 of 12 against none, and in DexHandExam 13 against 9 of 23. In the other direction, Sol is ahead of Astra in ManiGuard (5 against 3 of 7), RoboLab (2 against 0 of 6), iSpatialGym (5 against 4 of 8) and RoboDynamicExam (4 against 3 of 10). In Privileged, Sol leads Astra in BEHAVIOR-1K and MyoSuite but trails it in DexToolBench (10 against 13 of 20) and RoboDojo (6 against 10 of 12).

Many of these suites are small, and several leads are one test, so the single-test margins in DexToolBench, HumanoidBench and MuJoCo Playground are weak evidence by themselves. The larger gaps (RoboPaint, BEHAVIOR-1K Standard, RoboARC, ManiGuard) are more likely to reflect a real difference in how the agents work.

Some suites are mostly out of reach for every agent under real-robot rules, but almost none with full simulator access

Of the 22 suites with at least five Standard tests, 7 have at least half their tests solved by none of the five strong agents. With full simulator access, only one, HumanoidBench, is still that far out of reach.

The seven Standard suites, with the number of tests no strong agent solves, are HumanoidBench (7 of 8), the IKEA assembly tasks (7 of 8), DexToolBench (17 of 20), MyoSuite (8 of 13), BEHAVIOR-1K (36 of 71), RoboPaint (11 of 22) and RoboDojo (6 of 12). They cover different kinds of difficulty: whole-body humanoid control, furniture assembly and tool use with a dexterous hand, muscle-driven arms and hands, long household activities, painting that can only be checked through the cameras, and goals whose success check is written in different ways. In the two hardest, no strong agent solves more than two tests: Astra’s 2 is the most in DexToolBench, and in the IKEA assembly tasks only Fable and Astra solve one each.

This is the pattern the exam is designed to produce. In Privileged, the share of tests no strong agent solves falls to 4 of 20 in DexToolBench, 6 of 13 in MyoSuite, 19 of 71 in BEHAVIOR-1K, 1 of 12 in RoboDojo, and 0 in both RoboPaint (22 tests) and the IKEA assembly tasks (8 tests); only HumanoidBench stays at least half unsolved, at 4 of 8. So for six of the seven suites, exact state and unlimited resets let the strong agents find a solution, and what blocks them in Standard is doing the same work through cameras and sensors with few or no resets.

The 70% line shows the same from the other end. Averaged over the five strong agents, success reaches at least 70% in only one Standard suite, ManiGuard (71%), but in 12 of the 20 suites with at least five Privileged tests: ManiGuard, Embodied Olympics - Match, MuJoCo Playground, RoboDynamicExam, RoboCasa365, EmbodiedSWE, BiGym 2.0, RoboPaint, Embodied Olympics - Fun, DexHandExam, iSpatialGym and RoboARC.

Across all seven suites, the five weaker agents together solve two Standard tests, both by Kimi 3.1 Pro: a MyoSuite object reorientation that Fable, Astra and Opus also solve, and the RoboDojo block-swapping test that no other agent solves.

Every agent finds carrying objects easiest and using tools hardest, while the middle types change order

The paper gives each core task one or more of nine task types. Pooling the five strong agents, Object Transport has the highest Standard success rate, 45% of their attempts, and Tool Use the lowest, 14%. The order also holds for each agent separately: every one of the five does best on Object Transport and worst on Tool Use.

In Object Transport (104 tasks), the five agents solve between 38% and 64% of the Standard tests: Claude Fable 5.2 64%, GPT-6.1 Sol 43%, Gemini 4 Argon 39%, and GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 38% each. In Tool Use (50 tasks), Fable solves 24% (12 of 50), Astra 20% (10), Sol 12% (6), Argon 8% (4) and Opus 4% (2), so even the agent with the most successes fails three in four tool-use tests under real-robot rules. This matches the suite view: DexToolBench, where a dexterous hand must move a tool through goal poses, stays mostly unsolved.

Fable’s distance from the next agent varies by type. It is largest in Object Transport and Articulated Mechanisms, where Fable solves 64% and 47% and the other four between 20% and 43%, and smallest in the types that need precise contact, body control or tools: in Tool Use Astra is 4 points behind Fable (20% against 24%), in Precision & Contact 30% against 36%, and in Locomotion & Body Motion 26% against 32%. Astra and Sol tie in Dynamic Interaction (34% each) and Navigation & Search (28% each).

The middle of the order changes from agent to agent. Astra does better on Precision & Contact (30%) than on Articulated Mechanisms (24%), while Fable and Sol show the reverse. Argon solves more Dynamic Interaction (24%) and Precision & Contact (17%) tests than Opus (19% and 13%), while Opus is ahead on Navigation & Search (18% against 10%), Articulated Mechanisms (24% against 20%) and Deformable & Fluid (19% against 12%). Argon’s Navigation & Search rate, 10%, is close to its Tool Use rate.

Task types describe a whole task, and a hard type can be hard for several reasons. The stage-level failure labels, which assign a type to each stage, are the better tool for asking which step goes wrong (Where in a task do agents fail?).

Astra keeps checking and correcting until time runs out; Fable plans ahead and stops once it expects to miss

GPT-6 Astra keeps checking the robot and correcting it, from the cameras or the goal readout, until it succeeds or the time limit ends the attempt. Claude Fable 5.2 plans ahead, checks less often, and ends its session once its own estimate says the attempt will miss. Neither habit is better everywhere: checking catches small errors that pile up but costs time and steps, and planning ahead saves time but leaves nothing to catch a wrong plan or estimate.

The difference shows most clearly in the suites where Astra solves more Standard tests than Fable: RoboPaint, DexToolBench, HumanoidBench and MuJoCo Playground (counts in the suite table). GPT-6.1 Sol is also ahead of Fable in RoboPaint and level with it in DexToolBench and MuJoCo Playground. These suites are small and each test is one attempt, so the runs show the pattern more clearly than the counts.

We read both agents’ runs in RoboPaint, HumanoidBench and DexToolBench. The failures are the agents’ own, not faults of the tests.

HumanoidBench: the hard part is closing the loop from the cameras. Astra’s whole lead in this suite is one test, the cube manipulation, where a humanoid must rotate a cube above each palm to a goal orientation without dropping either. In the Standard Setting, Astra is the only one of the ten agents to solve it. The motion is within Fable’s reach: in the Realtime Setting both Fable and Astra solve it, and in the Privileged Setting six agents do, Fable among them. What the Standard Setting adds is that the cube’s pose has to come from the cameras.

The calligraphy tests show how thin the margin can be. On a kaishu copy, Fable used all 71 strokes, the last three as repairs, and the verifier measured an overlap with the target of 0.796 against the 0.80 required (run); Astra reached 0.90 (run). Which RoboPaint tests anyone solves depends strongly on the kind of picture. In Standard, 7 of the 10 acrylic paintings are solved by at least one agent (Sol solves 5 of them, Astra 3, Fable 1 and Opus 1), and both oil paintings are solved by several agents. Brush calligraphy is the opposite: of the 10 calligraphy tests, only 2 are solved in Standard, both by Astra, and none of the 6 running-script (xingshu) tests is solved by any agent. With the score visible in Privileged, every calligraphy test is solved by 5 to 8 agents. Writing the characters is within reach; judging stroke quality from the camera, without a score, is where the agents stop.

Across the core set, Fable stops early and Astra runs into the time limit. On the 230 Standard tests it fails, Fable ends its session after a median 67% of the time limit, and 180 of these attempts end with ten minutes or more left; Astra uses a median 97%, though it too ends 133 of its 299 failed attempts with ten minutes or more left, and Opus stops earlier still, at a median 45% (all three settings). Fable’s median Standard attempt takes 66 steps and 44 minutes, against Astra’s 138 steps and 56 minutes, and the time limit ends 1 of Fable’s Standard attempts against 92 of Astra’s. These figures are from our reading of the runs, the same data as the leaderboard.

Each habit has a cost. Often Fable’s unused time would not have helped, because it had used every reset, dropped the tool out of reach, or spent its strokes in paint that cannot be removed. Where its estimate was wrong, though, the time could have mattered: the kaishu copy missed by 0.004, and the spatula by 0.6 mm. Astra’s checking pays off on exactly these tests, but it also leaves 92 Standard attempts ended by the clock rather than by a result. Using more of the time limit does not by itself make an attempt succeed; what matters is whether the agent can tell from its own sensors how close it is.

Fable’s misses on widely solved tests come from an unwanted contact, a skipped check, or no margin left

When many agents solve a test that Claude Fable 5.2 fails, the failure shows what goes wrong for Fable on a task well within reach. Of the 230 Standard tests Fable fails, only 8 are solved by four or more of the other nine agents. In the five of these runs we read, Fable got the important part right and then lost the test on a contact it did not avoid, a check it did not make, or a margin it did not have.

The solvers are a mixed group. Weaker agents are among the solvers of 7 of the 8, and on the pour test four of the six solvers are weaker agents, while GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 fail it along with Fable. GPT-6 Astra solves 4 of the 8, Sol 6, and Opus and Gemini 4 Argon 7 each. Each test is one attempt per agent, so part of any single difference may be run variation.

In each of the five runs we read, the failure is Fable’s own, not a fault of the test or of the run, and it falls into one of three patterns. We do not cover the other three tests.

It touched what a safety rule protects. In two safety tests Fable made the right decision or reached the goal, and a contact broke the rule. On the pour test it read the white bottle’s BLEACH label and declined to pour it into ammonia, but while it brought the wrist camera in, the arm’s elbow toppled the open bottle and it spilled (below). On ManiGuard’s dusty transfer it cleaned the container and moved the food into it, but the gripper touched the food, which it had noted it could not rule out from its cameras (run). The four agents that solved the transfer made it without the contact.

Three runs of the same agent give success rates within five points of each other

The leaderboard counts one attempt per agent and test. To measure how much the success rate changes from one run to the next, the paper ran GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex on all 456 core Standard tests two more times at high effort, with everything else as in the leaderboard run.

The three runs’ success rates are within five percentage points of each other, with a mean of 30.8% and a standard deviation of 2.5 points, and each run by itself would rank third in the Standard column of the leaderboard.

Reasoning effort moves the result less than repeating the run: Sol at medium, high and maximum effort solves 30.7%, 32.2% and 30.9% of the Standard tests, a spread of 1.5 points (details).

When the simulation keeps running, the boundary moves further in, though not on every task

Realtime is harder than Standard for every strong agent. The simulation does not pause while the agent thinks, so a slow decision costs time on the robot. On the 54 core tasks that have both a Standard and a Realtime test, 25 of the 54 Realtime tests (46%) are solved by no agent, against 19 of the 54 Standard tests (35%).

On these 54 tasks, Claude Fable 5.2 solves 27 Standard tests and 22 Realtime tests, GPT-6 Astra 23 and 17, GPT-6.1 Sol 17 and 13, Claude Opus 5.5 10 and 4, and Gemini 4 Argon 15 and 5. Opus and Argon lose the most, and Realtime is the one setting where Argon solves more than Opus, by one test. Kimi 3.1 Pro solves 4 Realtime tests, as many as Opus; Gemini 3.8 Flash, Qwen3.8 Max and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash solve 1 each, and GLM 5.3 Flash none.

The drop is not uniform, and some tasks go better in Realtime. Of the 54 tasks, 12 are solved in Standard by some agent but in Realtime by none, and 6 the other way round. Every strong agent except Argon solves some Realtime tests it fails in Standard: Fable 9, Astra 3, Sol 3 and Opus 2. The suites differ. In BiGym 2.0, 8 of 10 Realtime tests stay unsolved, against 3 of 10 in Standard, and in Embodied Olympics - Match 5 of 12 against 2 of 12. In RoboDynamicExam the direction reverses: 1 of 8 Realtime tests stays unsolved, against 3 of 8 in Standard, and Fable solves 6 Realtime tests there against 3 Standard tests. In Realtime, 97% of the sampled attempts control the robot with a program, most of them one that runs the control loop (control approach), so tasks that reward a fast closed loop can gain from this setting.

The two-way comparisons between agents shift as well. In Realtime, 9 tests are solved only by Fable and 4 only by Astra, with 13 solved by both and 28 by neither. Sol solves 5 Realtime tests that Fable fails, one more than Astra, while Astra solves 7 that Sol fails and Sol 3 that Astra fails. With only 54 tests, each of these differences rests on a handful of attempts.

What information matters to the agent? Privileged versus Standard

The three settings differ only in what the agent can see and do

The three tests of a task share the initial scene, the success rule and the time limit, and a verifier outside the agent’s control grades every attempt. Only the agent’s access to the world changes (paper Figure 3, below).

In the Standard Setting, the agent runs in its own container and reaches the robot only through the robot’s interface: it requests camera images, depth and joint readings, submits chunks of actions, and must estimate poses from images and depth. It never gets object poses, segmentation, contact forces or the success checker, and it cannot reset the scene unless the task grants a reset budget (nine suites do, with 3 to 50 resets). The simulator and the verifier share a second container and grade the live episode as it runs, so an error stays in the graded result. The simulation pauses while the agent thinks. The Realtime Setting is the same, except that the simulation never waits.

In the Privileged Setting, the agent shares a container with the simulator. It can read the exact state of every object (pose, size, velocity, joint positions), step the physics itself, call planning primitives, reset the scene without limit, and save and restore states. It submits a low-level trajectory (for some tasks, a controller program), which a separate verifier replays on a fresh copy of the scene, so mistakes made while exploring cost nothing. The time limit is the same as in Standard: Privileged gives more access, not more time. The tasks are designed so that this access is enough to find a solution by search; a high Privileged rate shows that a task is solvable, and the paper treats it as an approximate upper bound.

The tasks are also designed to stay hard under real-robot rules, so a high Privileged rate with a low Standard rate is the intended result. The gap between them measures what an agent loses when it has to work like a real robot. The sections below measure how much Privileged success survives in Standard, and whether exact state or retries account for the gap.

Full simulator access hides much of what makes a task hard

On the 391 core tasks with both a Standard and a Privileged test, every agent fails in Standard a large share of the tasks it solves in Privileged: Claude Fable 5.2 fails 137 of 312 (44%), GPT-6 Astra 192 of 308 (62%), GPT-6.1 Sol 176 of 286 (62%), Claude Opus 5.5 147 of 233 (63%) and Gemini 4 Argon 106 of 167 (63%). For the five weaker agents the share runs from 81% (Qwen3.8 Max, 30 of 37) to 92% (GLM 5.3 Flash, 35 of 38). A Privileged success shows that an agent can find a solution in principle, not that it can do the task through the robot’s cameras (Figure 11).

The reverse is rare. Tasks solved in Standard but not in Privileged number 9 for Fable, 4 for Astra, 5 for Sol and 5 for Opus; Gemini 4 Argon has 18, the most of any agent. Privileged is therefore an upper bound over a set of tasks, not for every single task.

The drop is broad. The paper reports that Fable’s success rate drops in all but one of the suites with at least five such tasks; in the site data, that suite is iSpatialGym, where Fable solves 7 of 8 tasks in both settings. Astra and Sol drop in every one of these suites. Opus does not drop in ManiGuard (7 of 7 in both) or DexToolBench (none in either). Argon shows small reversals in MyoSuite (1 of 13 in Standard, 0 in Privileged) and RoboDojo (4 of 12 in Standard, 3 in Privileged), and ties in RoboLab (1 of 6 in both).

Agents that look even in Privileged can differ a lot in Standard. Of the 391 Privileged tests, only Fable solves 28, only Astra 24, both 284 and neither 55. In Standard, Fable keeps 175 of its Privileged successes and Astra 116 (Figure 11). What separates them is how much survives without exact state and free retries, the two features the next sections take apart.

Being able to retry helps more than knowing where everything is

When the paper adds the Privileged features to the Standard interface one at a time, unlimited resets close more of the gap than exact object state. This suggests, as an inference rather than a proven claim, that the harder part for the agent is getting the action right, not knowing where things are.

The paper ran GPT-6 Astra in Codex with the reasoning effort, time limit and tasks of its leaderboard run, changing only these features. The state query is read-only and returns, for every object, its position, orientation, size and bounding box, linear and angular velocity, and the joint positions of the robot and of articulated objects such as doors and drawers. It never returns success flags, scores, thresholds or goal regions, and it hides fields the agent is meant to find out itself, such as goal markers, hint notes and hazard labels. The reset feature raises the reset budget to 1,000 and adds calls that save the simulator state under a handle and restore it later. Runs are scored on the default test of each of the 391 core tasks that have both settings (the Standard test, or the Realtime test for 50 of them), so the 29% baseline is the leaderboard run counted on this set, not Astra’s 157 of 456 Standard tests.

Astra solves 29% with the unmodified interface, 45% with the state query, 53% with unlimited resets and saved states, and 68% with both, still 11 points short of the 79% it reaches on the Privileged tests of the same tasks (Figure 12a). On the 50 Realtime tests, the gap between the two features is wider: 30% at baseline, 38% with the state query, 64% with resets, 80% with both and 84% in Privileged (paper Table 5).

Resets are used to retry hard stages, not to start over. Of the reset run’s 206 successes, only 14% came in the first episode, and 136 (66%) came in an episode that started from a saved mid-task state. The agent returns to just before a hard stage and tries it again without repeating the whole task. A real robot in the Standard Setting cannot do this: one slip at a hard stage usually ends the graded episode.

Exact state helps where cameras struggle. The paper finds the state query helps most where the scene is hard to measure from cameras, as in iSpatialGym and BEHAVIOR-1K, and changes little in Embodied Olympics (Fun) and DexHandExam. Seeing the scene is a real problem in some suites; across the whole set, it is the smaller of the two.

Limits. Each condition is a single run of a single agent, so differences of a few points may be run variation, and other agents may show a different order. Neither added feature lets the agent step the physics or call planning primitives, as Privileged does, and the paper notes these abilities may account for part of the remaining 11 points.

The drop concentrates at stages that need a tight fit, a target pose or a moving contact

The stages that fail much more often in Standard than in Privileged are the ones that need precision. Among the stage types compared in at least two suites, the failure rate rises by 19 to 51 percentage points at precise types, but by only 3 to 18 points at seven of the nine coarse types (Figure 12b, above).

The paper splits stage types into two groups by their definitions. A stage is precise if it needs a tight fit, a mated or threaded relation, a target pose or path, or contact with a moving object; the 13 precise types are insert, assemble, screw or unscrew, thread or hang, connect, adjust pose, follow path, stabilize, regrasp, throw, strike, catch and dribble. A stage is coarse if it only needs to reach a region, a container, a grasp or the open or closed state of a mechanism; the 12 coarse types are place, put in, take out, gather, pick up, push, pull, open, close, press, switch on or off, and navigate. Material stages such as cleaning or pouring, and body stages other than navigate, are in neither group. A type’s failure rate in a setting is the number of its stages at which attempts fail, divided by the number that attempts reach, compared within each suite.

The precise types rise as follows: assemble by 51 points, adjust pose and catch by 37, screw or unscrew by 35, strike by 33, insert by 32, follow path by 21, thread or hang by 20 and connect by 19. Among the coarse types, open rises by 18, put in by 17, pick up by 14, place by 13, switch on or off by 6, press by 4 and close by 3; the exceptions are take out (42 points) and navigate (27 points). Pooled within suites, the failure rate at precise stages is 45% in Standard against 16% in Privileged, across 13 suites, and at coarse stages 32% against 18%, across 25 suites. With suite fixed effects, precise stages rise by 15 points more than coarse ones (95% interval 9 to 21).

The paired check. Different attempts may reach different kinds of stages, so the paper also counts only the stages that the same agent reaches on the same task in both settings. There, the failure rate rises by 22.8 points at precise stages and 13.4 points at coarse stages, a smaller but still significant difference of 9.4 points (95% interval 4.7 to 14.5). Follow-path stages, most of them DexToolBench waypoints, make up 39% of these precise stages and rise by only 12 points; without them, precise stages rise by 29.6 points, 16.2 more than coarse ones (95% interval 10.4 to 22.5). Striking, assembling, adjusting a pose and catching rise by 30 to 45 points, while closing, placing and picking up rise by 2 to 15.

A precise stage that fails in Standard. In a DexHandExam task, a dexterous hand must seat the front guard of a fan on its rear guard and close four clips; the two upper clips need the guard to be seated. In Standard, Claude Opus 5.5 held the guard 36 to 39 degrees off the fan’s axis, where seating allows at most 5 degrees; pushing the guard on moved the whole fan, and the episode ended at 24 s (Figure 13). In Privileged, it closed the lower clips, seated the guard at 20 s and closed the upper clips. In the same Standard test, GPT-6 Astra seated the guard twice, each time for less than 2 s: as the hand withdrew its fingers, the guard came off with it and reopened a lower clip, and the attempt ended when the guard dropped. In the site data, five of the ten agents (Fable, Astra, Sol, Opus and Kimi 3.1 Pro) solve this task in Privileged and none in Standard.

What the labels can and cannot say. The paper’s labels attribute the Opus failure to a wrong estimate of the guard’s pose in the hand, but stage labels cannot split perception from control: a precise stage can fail because the pose was misjudged, because the motion was executed badly, or both. The labels show where the Standard Setting makes tasks harder; the controlled runs in the next section separate exact state from retries.

At precise stages, seeing the exact pose barely helps on its own; retrying the action does

Adding the state query barely rescues failures at precise stages, so not seeing the exact pose is not the main cause. At precise stages, exact state solves 21% of GPT-6 Astra’s failures, about the same as a plain second try (19%), while unlimited resets solve 36%. At coarse stages the order flips: exact state solves 34% of the failures, against 14% for a second try.

The paper joins the controlled runs to Astra’s labeled failures on the default tests. Of these failures, 256 have a located failure stage: 91 at precise stages, 97 at coarse stages, 36 at material stages and 32 at other stages. Some failures would pass simply on a second attempt, so each run is compared with a second-try baseline: the run that adds neither feature and only hides the success signal (next section). It is not an exact repeat, since the agent must end the episode itself, but its overall success rate is within 4 points of the original run’s.

At precise stages, resets carry the gain. Resets solve 36% of the 91 precise failures, almost twice the second try, and 31 of those 33 successes came only after a reset or a restored state. The difference from exact state is unlikely to be chance: of the 22 precise failures that exactly one of the state run and the reset run solves, the reset run solves 18 (exact McNemar test, p = 0.004). Exact state does help once retries are available: with both features, the share rises from 36% to 47% (of 16 failures that only one of these two runs solves, the run with both solves 13, p = 0.021).

At coarse stages, exact state matters. Of the 97 coarse failures, exact state alone solves 34% and resets alone 44%. With both, the agent solves 67%, in line with the 62% that Privileged solves on the same tasks: for stages that only need a region, a container or a grasp, the two added features already reach roughly what full simulator access gives.

At precise stages, a gap to Privileged remains. With both features, the agent solves 47% of the precise failures, against 74% in Privileged. On the precise failures that have a Privileged test, the gain of the run with both over the second try is 52% of the gain that Privileged achieves (95% interval 34% to 69%). Privileged solves 24 more precise failures than the run with both, 16 of them at follow-path, thread and assemble stages; at striking and catching stages, the two solve exactly the same failures. The remaining gap may come from stepping the physics one step at a time and calling planning primitives, which neither added feature provides; this suggests that precise stages benefit from testing a motion before running it in a graded attempt.

Limits. This is one run per condition, of GPT-6 Astra only. Precise and coarse failures also come from different suites: most precise failures are in Embodied Olympics (Fun), DexToolBench and RoboARC, while 38 of the 97 coarse failures are in BEHAVIOR-1K. The paper reports that leaving out any one suite reverses none of the comparisons, but its intervals and tests treat the failed tests as independent.

GPT-6 Astra barely depends on being told that the task has succeeded

Hiding the success signal barely changes how often GPT-6 Astra succeeds: on the default tests of the 391 core tasks with both settings, it solves 31% without the signal against 29% with it, and on the 50 Realtime tests, 26% against 30%. Exact state and, above all, the ability to retry matter far more.

In the normal Standard interface, the simulator reports when an episode has succeeded and ends it at that moment. In the no-signal run, no success flag, progress or score is returned, and the episode does not end when the success rule is met. The agent has to judge that it is done and call finish, or the episode ends when time runs out or the task’s own failure rule triggers; the success rule is then checked on the final state. This is stricter in one way: a success that was reached and then undone, for example an object placed correctly and then knocked off, counts as a failure.

What this tells us. If agents relied on the signal to know when to stop, or to find a solution by trying moves until the flag appeared, hiding it should have cost many tests. The change, 2 points on the default tests and 4 on the Realtime tests, is within the run-to-run variation the paper reports (up to 5 points across three runs of GPT-6.1 Sol). When Astra reaches the goal, it usually knows it has. The information that limits it is not a verdict at the end of the episode but the ability to go back and retry a hard stage and, at coarse stages, an exact view of where things are.

Why this run serves as a second try. Because hiding the signal changes so little, the paper uses this run as a rough stand-in for running each test again, the baseline in the precise and coarse comparison above. The paper notes that this is approximate: the run does not separate ordinary variation between attempts from the effect of hiding the signal, and it is a single run of a single agent.

Where in a task do agents fail?

When several agents fail a task, they tend to fail at the same stage, so the hard step belongs to the task more than to the agent

Each task is divided into stages, each a change in the world that the task requires: the fridge is open, the tomato is inside, the fridge is closed again. The failure analysis records the stage at which each failed attempt went wrong, so we can ask whether two agents that fail the same test fail at the same point. Mostly they do.

The paper measures this as the concentration of failure stages: the chance that two failed attempts on a test, picked at random, failed at the same stage (they always come from different agents, since each agent makes one attempt per test). On the Standard tests that at least two agents failed at located stages, on tasks with at least two stages, the concentration averages 58% over the ten agents. If each failure fell at a random stage, with early stages favoured in the same proportion as on the other tasks, it would be 35% (Figure 14, panel (a)). In the Privileged Setting it is 49% against 32%.

The weaker agents do not drive this number, although they often fail before meeting any stage and two failures at the very first stage agree trivially. Among the five strong agents alone, on the Standard tests where at least two of them failed at located stages, the concentration is 55%, against 30% for a random baseline built from their own failures on the other Standard tasks.

The agreement is also stronger than the kinds of stages alone predict. In a second baseline, each agent fails at each stage at the failure rate of that stage’s type on the other tasks, so a hard type of stage (wiping, say) attracts failures wherever it appears. This baseline gives 44% in Standard, still well below the observed 58%: particular stages of particular tasks are hard for everyone. Of the Standard tests with at least three stages and four or more located failures, 51 of 192 have at least 80% of their failures on a single stage. Examples are putting the first branch into the bin when cleaning up branches and twigs (BEHAVIOR-1K, 10 of 10 failures), putting the second spice jar onto a tray (Embodied Safety Exam, 9 of 9), and seating the front guard of a fan (DexHandExam, 8 of 9).

The shared stage is often the first one. Of the 261 Standard tests whose failures have a single most common stage, that stage is the task’s first stage in 64%, and in 54% of the tests whose tasks have at least two kinds of stages. Failing at the same stage does not mean failing in the same way: two agents that failed at the same Standard stage share a failure phenomenon (such as a lost hold or a collision) in only 39% of pairs.

Catching a ball twice (RoboDynamicExam) shows a shared stage that is not the first (Figure 15). A Franka arm holding a cup must catch a thrown ball, pour it into a slot, catch it again after it rolls down and rebounds off a ramp, and then pour it into a moving cart. In the Standard Setting all ten agents failed, and eight of them caught the first ball and then failed at the recatch, in different ways. In four attempts (GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 4 Argon, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Qwen3.8 Max) the cup touched the slot or the rebound ramp while pouring the ball out or moving downstream, which the task forbids and which ends the episode. GPT-6.1 Sol poured the ball into the slot by about 10 s, but at 16.7 s, as it adjusted the cup for the rebounding ball, the arm touched the scene. Claude Opus 5.5’s cup was still too high when the ball came off the ramp; Opus itself reported that the ball can be caught for only about 1.3 to 1.6 s after it leaves the cup, while straightening the cup after the pour alone takes about 1.4 s. In the runs of DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM 5.3 Flash the ball never left the tilted cup. The two attempts that did not stop at the recatch went opposite ways: Kimi 3.1 Pro did not move the arm and failed at the first catch after about 1 s, while Claude Fable 5.2 recaught the ball at 13.6 s and got to the last stage, where the ball fell beside the moving cart at 18.2 s.

This bottleneck comes mostly from timing and sensing under real-robot rules rather than from understanding the task. In the Realtime Setting, the task’s default test, Claude Fable 5.2 and GPT-6.1 Sol passed, and all seven located failures of the other agents were again at the recatch. In the Privileged Setting, five agents completed all three stages: Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5 and also Kimi 3.1 Pro, which had not moved the arm in Standard. Gemini 4 Argon, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Qwen3.8 Max submitted no answer there, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM 5.3 Flash failed at the cart and at the recatch.

Full simulator access changes what happens at and after the hard stage, not where the hard stage is

Agents solve far more tests in the Privileged Setting, yet the stage at which most failures of a task fall is usually the same in Standard and Privileged. The paper takes the 186 tasks whose failures have a single most common stage in both settings. In 150 of them, or 81%, that stage is the same, against 47% if the failure stages of each setting were drawn independently at random, with early stages favoured as before. Much of this agreement comes from tasks that simply fail at their first stage: in 98 of the 186 tasks, the common stage is the first stage in both settings. On the remaining 88 tasks the common stage is still the same in 59% (95% interval 49% to 69%), against 9% under the random baseline with draws where both common stages are the first stage left out (Figure 14, panel (a), bottom bar).

Where the two common stages differ, the Standard one usually comes earlier in the task (in 25 tasks, against 11 where it comes later): under real-robot rules, agents get stuck sooner.

The settings differ sharply after the hard stage. For each attempt, the paper takes the stage at which the other agents on the same test most often failed and asks whether the attempt got past it. Of the attempts that did, 43% passed the test in Standard, against 71% in Privileged; most of the other Standard attempts failed at a later stage. Over all attempts, the pass rates are 15% and 39%. Part of the link between getting past the stage and passing comes from stage order, since such an attempt is already closer to the end of the task. Even so, the gap between 43% and 71% says that in Standard, clearing the hardest step is often not enough: later stages that full access makes easy can still fail.

So the drop from Privileged to Standard is not mainly agents taking a different route through the task and hitting new obstacles. The same steps are hard in both settings, and real-robot rules make them harder and add failures after them. These counts describe where failures fall; they do not show that an agent follows the same plan in the two settings.

Changing a material or moving the robot’s body fails most often, but which kinds of stage are hardest does not carry over from one suite to another

Every stage has a type naming the robot action that brings it about, and the types fall into categories: relation (placing or fitting one object relative to another), pose, mechanism (doors, lids, buttons), material (wiping, pouring, painting), body (walking, keeping balance), dynamic (catching, striking) and competition. A category’s failure rate is the share of its stages that attempts reach and then fail. Pooled over Standard and Privileged, the rates spread widely (Figure 14, panel (b)): material stages fail at 56% (741 of 1,313 reached), body stages at 39% (474 of 1,228), competition and relation stages at 29% each, dynamic stages at 28%, pose stages at 19% and mechanism stages at 17% (387 of 2,330).

The extremes hold in each setting separately, and every category fails more often under real-robot rules: material stages at 75% in Standard and 35% in Privileged, body stages at 58% and 23%, and mechanism stages at 22% and 11%. The pose, relation and dynamic categories lie between, in an order that differs between the settings, so the paper does not rank these three against each other.

Within a category, how the robot has to act matters as much as what it acts on. Among mechanism stages, opening a door or a lid fails at 31% (282 of 907), closing one at 15% when it is not already closed at the start, and pressing at 3% (14 of 435). Stages done by pressing or wiping fail at 6% when the robot presses on a mechanism such as a button, and at 62% when it wipes or presses on a material. Relation stages fail at 29% of reached stages with one hand or gripper, at 43% when two arms act together, and at 59% when the legs or torso carry the robot’s weight (paper Figure 28). Acting through a tool cuts both ways: stages on moving objects fail less often when the robot acts through a cup or a racket, while pose stages fail more often when it moves an object through a tool.

These rates are associations, not causes, for three reasons. First, each category occurs mostly in particular suites and with particular robots: legs and torso belong to legged robots, and most material failures come from RoboPaint and Embodied Safety Exam, so a high material rate may say as much about those two suites as about materials. Second, stages differ in how much the robot must do. A relation stage usually covers grasping, carrying and releasing the object, because the task graphs list picking up as a stage of its own in only 11 of the 229 tasks with relation stages, so a rate per stage compares units of different size. Third, the taxonomy records what a stage changes, not how much contact it needs.

A difficulty ranking learned in some suites mostly does not predict difficulty in a new one. The paper holds out one suite at a time, ranks each pair of stage types by their failure rates on the other suites, and checks the ranking inside the held-out suite. It holds for 55% of 361 pairs of stage types, barely above the 50% of a coin flip; for the mode of contact alone, 48% of 163 pairs; for the coarser stage categories, 65% of 82 pairs.

What does predict failure at a stage is who is acting. The paper predicts whether an attempt that reaches a stage fails there from the setting plus one other attribute, with cross-validation over tasks, and reports how much each attribute improves on the setting alone: the agent by 15%, the stage type by 6.0%, the contact (who touches and how) by 6.0%, the stage category by 4.7%, the suite by 4.3% and the kind of robot body by 3.2%. So a specific stage of a specific task is often a shared bottleneck, but the general kind of that stage is a weak guide to difficulty elsewhere (with the caveat that different models wrote the labels of different agents).

Each suite fails in its own way, because the suite fixes which kinds of stages, bodies and dangers an agent meets

Broken down by suite, the 5,517 analyzed failures show that the suite largely decides where and how agents fail (Figure 26).

The failure stage is often fixed by the suite’s design. Every RoboPaint failure is at a material stage, every Embodied Olympics - Match failure at a competition stage, and every DexToolBench failure with a located stage at a pose stage. In Embodied Olympics - Match and DexToolBench this follows from the tasks, since every stage of every task there is of that category. Elsewhere one category dominates without being the only one: dynamic stages hold 63% of RoboDynamicExam failures and 58% of Embodied Olympics - Fun failures, body stages 93% of MuJoCo Playground failures, and pose stages 73% of MyoSuite failures. Relation stages dominate the household and tabletop suites: 54% of BEHAVIOR-1K failures, 68% of RoboARC and BiGym 2.0, 87% of RoboDojo, 89% of KinDER, 91% of RoboCasa365 and all of RoboLab’s. Over all failures, relation stages are the most common, at 38%.

How far a failed attempt gets also differs sharply. In DexToolBench and KinDER, most failed attempts never meet a single stage of the task (69% and 77%), and in BiGym 2.0 51% meet none: the agent never establishes the first grasp or tool pose. In RoboPaint, 95% of failed attempts meet at least half of the stages, because painting fails on how well the strokes match, not on whether the robot begins. In Embodied Safety Exam and ManiGuard, some failed attempts meet every stage at one moment (14% and 22%) and still fail; in Embodied Safety Exam most of these break a safety constraint.

The same kind of stage can fail for different reasons in different suites. RoboLab and RoboCasa365 both fail mostly at relation stages, but RoboLab’s most common phenomenon is a lost hold (49%) and RoboCasa365’s a failure to make the contact the stage needs (39%). Losing balance or gait dominates where the robot carries its own weight: 82% of MuJoCo Playground failures, 49% of HumanoidBench and 35% of Embodied Olympics - Fun. It is also a third of BEHAVIOR-1K failures (33%), where a mobile manipulator can tip over while reaching or carrying. Embodied Safety Exam stands out for collisions (35%) and constraint violations (25%), which a safety suite is built to catch. In RoboDynamicExam, inaccurate poses (24%), collisions (22%) and timing errors (14%) all appear, matching the cup-and-ball failures above. DexToolBench’s most common phenomenon is a failure to make contact (45%).

Root causes point mostly to control, but support only broad conclusions. A control root cause appears in 69% of all failures and a cognitive one in 12%. Among suites with at least 20 failures, cognitive causes are most frequent in Embodied Safety Exam (36%), RoboPaint (33%) and iSpatialGym (27%), where the agent must read a rule, a target image or a spatial instruction correctly. In eleven suites with at least 20 failures, among them HumanoidBench, MyoSuite and DexToolBench, they stay under 8%. The labels cannot cleanly separate perception from control (see the limitations), and 19% of all failures carry no root cause, so we read this block as showing where cognition clearly matters, not as a measured split between thinking and acting.

The robot’s body shapes what goes wrong: robots that carry their own weight fall, and hands fail to make contact

Figure 27 breaks the same 5,517 failures down by the kind of robot body, by agent and by setting. Each kind of body comes from only a few suites (three quarters of the mobile-manipulator failures are BEHAVIOR-1K’s, and all quadruped failures come from MuJoCo Playground), and the bodies differ in their mix of settings, so these rows describe failures on the tasks each body appears in, not the effect of the body itself.

Robots that support their own weight mostly fail by falling. Loss of balance or a gait failure is the most common phenomenon for quadrupeds (88% of their failures), humanoids (32%) and mobile manipulators (25%). Every quadruped failure is at a body stage. Humanoid failures are spread more widely, with 35% at dynamic stages and 28% at body stages, because many humanoid tasks come from the Embodied Olympics.

Hands often fail before they get going. Failure to make the contact a stage needs is the most common phenomenon for dexterous hands (32%) and for the musculoskeletal models of MyoSuite (34%). For dexterous hands, 55% of failed attempts never meet a stage of the task, and 69% of failures are at pose stages: the hand never reaches the grasp or tool pose that the rest of the task depends on.

Arms fail on accuracy and clearance. For robots with one or two arms, the most common phenomena apart from task-specific defects are inaccurate poses (20% for one arm, 24% for two) and collisions or obstructions (18% and 19%). Two-armed robots fail at relation stages in 69% of cases, where both arms must place or thread an object together; one-armed robots fail at material stages in 37%, mostly painting.

The agents differ in how far they get and what goes wrong, not in which kind of stage stops them. For every agent, relation stages hold the largest share of failures, from 33% (Qwen3.8 Max) to 43% (GPT-6.1 Sol), and the other categories differ by about five points or less between agents. The weaker agents’ failures more often meet no stage of the task (26% to 37%, against 15% to 23% for the five strong agents) and more often lack the needed contact (per contact stage reached, see Weaker agents). A lost hold makes up a larger share of the failures of Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol (17% each) than of any weaker agent (7% to 12%), partly because an agent that reaches more grasp stages has more chances to lose a hold: per grasp stage reached, GPT-6 Astra loses the hold in 5.4% of cases against 6.8% for the other nine agents pooled (the interval includes zero). Among the strong agents, the share of failures whose progress could not be read ranges from 5% (Claude Fable 5.2) and 6% (GPT-6.1 Sol) to 10% (Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 4 Argon) and 11% (GPT-6 Astra), and Gemini 4 Argon’s failures more often carry the not-attempted label (15%, the highest of the ten). Kimi 3.1 Pro (49%) and Gemini 4 Argon (42%) have many failures with no root cause, but the two label writers leave the root cause empty at different rates, so root-cause shares should not be compared between agents.

The settings change the kind of failure in ways that match their rules. Realtime failures more often lie at competition stages (22%, against 2% in Standard and in Privileged) and dynamic stages (25%, against 9% and 8%), and more often involve a timing error (16%, against 2% and 1%), because in Realtime the world keeps moving while the agent thinks. Collisions are more common in Standard failures (14%, against 5% in Realtime and 7% in Privileged), losses of balance are more common in Standard (17%) and Realtime (18%) than in Privileged (11%), and Privileged failures more often carry the not-attempted label (14%, against 3% in Standard and 9% in Realtime). The settings cover different mixes of tests, so these rows do not isolate the effect of the setting either.

The weaker agents do not fail the same steps more often than the strong ones; they fail earlier. Their attempts more often end before the task has started, and at stages that need contact they miss the contact several times more often. Almost everything a weaker agent solves, some strong agent also solves.

Many weaker-agent attempts meet no stage at all. Among failed Standard attempts on tasks with at least two stages, panel (a) counts those that met no stage: 44% (Kimi 3.1 Pro) to 64% (Qwen3.8 Max, 114 of 178) for the five weaker agents, against 26% to 36% for the five strong agents. Fable rarely fails before touching the object; when it fails, it is usually after contact, with a misjudged pose or a lost grip

Claude Fable 5.2 almost always makes the contact a stage needs, so its failures come later, while it holds or places an object: an inaccurate pose (18.3% of its failed attempts) and a lost hold (17.5%) are its two most common failure phenomena. The numbers come from the paper’s failure profile of each agent (Table 20 in its appendix; Figure 16(a) shows four of its rows), over failed attempts in the Standard and Realtime Settings; a failed attempt can have two phenomena, so the shares do not sum to 100%.

It almost always makes the contact a stage needs. Only 5.7% of Fable’s failed attempts fail to make the required contact, against 9.9% for GPT-6 Astra and 12.5% for GPT-6.1 Sol. Per contact stage reached, Fable fails without contact at 1.0%, the lowest of the ten agents (the other strong agents 2.7% to 3.7%). Obstructions or collisions (11.8% against 16.9% for Astra) and constraint violations (3.7% against 6.7%) also make up a smaller share of its failures.

A lost hold is common in its failures, but per grasp it is rare. A lost hold makes up 17.5% of Fable’s failed attempts, second only to Sol (20.2%) among the ten agents, against 16.9% for Astra and 12.6% for Claude Opus 5.5. Per grasp stage reached, Fable loses the hold in 4.1% of cases and Astra in 5.4%; only Fable and Gemini 4 Argon (4.4%) are below Astra, and Sol is the highest of the strong agents, at 7.0%. The paper’s rope-threading case shows a lost hold. Threading a rope through five passages, Fable threaded the first three by about 91 s, but as it regripped the rope end for the second ring, the rope slipped out of the gripper at 99 s and again at 125 s. Some of its failures come from giving up or exploring, which Astra’s rarely do. In 3.7% of Fable’s failed attempts the step was not attempted, against 0.3% for Astra and 0.6% for Sol, and 6.1% failed while the agent was still exploring the scene, against 3.2% for Astra. Zero progress is the same for the two, 26% of their failed Standard attempts on tasks with at least two stages.

When both fail, Astra and Fable get about equally far. On the tests with at least three stages that both failed, leaving out ties, Astra met more stages than Fable on 39 of 71 tests, or 55%, against 73% to 100% of such tests for every other agent. Fable met more stages than Opus on 76% of 78 such tests. So Fable’s failures are not earlier than Astra’s; what differs is mainly where in a stage they go wrong.

What these labels can and cannot show. Labels from replay video and logs often cannot tell a wrong estimate from a wrong motion, so an “inaccurate pose” may be a misjudged object or a misplaced hand. The root-cause layer supports few conclusions, so we compare phenomena and stages here, not root causes. Each agent has one labeled attempt per test, so shares on a few dozen tests have wide uncertainty.

Which control approach do agents use?

The setting decides how agents act: the model drives the robot in Standard, and programs drive it in Realtime and Privileged

An agent can control the robot in three ways, or mix them. In direct control, the model itself is the controller: it sends a move, looks at the camera images or sensor readings, and chooses the next move. In code to control, it writes a planner, a controller or another program, and the program produces the robot’s actions. In train a policy, it trains a policy in simulation and lets the policy control the robot. The figure shows one attempt of each kind, with an excerpt of what the agent wrote.

In a random sample of attempts, agents controlled the robot directly in 63% of the Standard attempts and wrote programs to control it in 97% of the Realtime attempts and 79% of the Privileged attempts. The reason is the clock. In Standard the simulation waits while the model thinks, so the model can sit inside the control loop and choose each move after it looks. In Realtime the simulation keeps running, so a program has to run the loop. In Privileged the agent has the full simulator state and can compute or search for a motion before it submits it.

What the sample covers. A separate coding agent read the logs of the 1,760 attempts and labeled each with the approach that produced its graded answer. The attempts were sampled at random from the runs of 18 October 2026 and, for GPT-6.1 Sol, from its runs of 15 November 2026 (289 attempts). The labels are an agent’s reading of the logs, not a hand annotation, so small differences between shares are not meaningful.

Standard: the model is the controller. Of the 859 Standard attempts, 63% used direct control: the model sends one chunk of actions, looks at the camera images or sensor readings, and chooses the next chunk while the robot waits. Only 30% wrote a program to control the robot (8% open-loop, 13% closed-loop, 8% parameter search). Another 6% mixed approaches and 1% had no control plan.

Realtime: a program runs the loop. Of the 115 Realtime attempts, 97% used a program and none used direct control. Most programs are closed-loop, reading the robot’s state or cameras while they run: 81% of all Realtime attempts, against 13% open-loop programs and 3% parameter searches. In the paper’s badminton example from Embodied Olympics - Match, a program written by GPT-6 Astra ran in the background, located the shuttlecock with two cameras, fitted a drag model to its flight and swung the racket. Whenever play paused, the agent read the program’s logs and revised it, and it won 11 to 4 against an opponent that picks its shots with a learned policy.

Privileged: compute or search first, then submit. Of the 786 Privileged attempts, 79% used a program: 19% open-loop, 43% closed-loop and 17% parameter search. The parameter searches, 136 of the 624 programs, try many episodes in the simulator until a motion passes. Direct control falls to 12%, and 6% of the attempts had no control plan.

The same agent changes approach when the setting changes. On a RoboTwin 2.0 task that asks for three bottles to go into a dustbin, GPT-6 Astra in the Standard Setting sent end-effector commands one at a time and checked the camera images after each. It knocked one bottle over, picked it up again with the other arm, and finished about 30 seconds before the time limit. In the Privileged Setting it read the task’s source code, scripted the grasps with the simulator’s motion primitives, handed the bottles from one arm to the other in mid-air and submitted after five minutes.

Training a policy is rare unless the test supplies the tools. Only 7 of the 1,760 sampled attempts trained a policy: 0.6% of the Standard sample, 2% of the Realtime sample and none of the Privileged sample. Many Privileged tests give the agent a GPU to run the simulator, but their instructions name no training library. Apart from these, only eight tests give the agent a GPU: the GPU versions of a soccer match and of a table tennis match in each setting, and two RoboEngineer tests that ask the agent to train a walking policy. When a test does supply a GPU and a training library, agents train, as the soccer match below shows.

Given a GPU and a training library, agents switch from hand-written gaits to trained policies, and in Standard and Realtime almost every win comes from a policy they trained

In the Standard and Realtime settings of a humanoid soccer match, agents trained a policy when given a GPU and a training library, and 8 of the 9 wins used a trained policy. Without the GPU, agents wrote walking gaits by hand and won once. In the Privileged Setting, 7 of the 9 wins instead reused the opponent’s walking network.

Two versions of one match. The soccer match of Embodied Olympics - Match comes in two versions with the same frozen scene and the same opponent, a team that plays with a learned policy. Each team has two Booster T1 humanoids, and the limit is four hours. The agent controls its two robots joint by joint, so it needs a walking gait before it can play at all. The CPU version has no GPU and no machine-learning framework, and its instruction allows learning code in NumPy or SciPy.

With the GPU, agents trained. In the Standard and Realtime settings, 25 of the 28 GPU tests trained a policy with reinforcement learning, and 22 of these 25 played the match with it (Figure 18, panel (b), above). One agent, two versions. In the Standard Setting, GPT-6 Astra in Codex shows the gap within a single agent. With the GPU, it trained walking and ball-control policies, combined them with hand-written tactics and won 10 to 8; its robots walked 690 m against the opponent’s 693 m and touched the ball 150 times against 142. Counting only each agent’s own harness, the 20 GPU tests trained in 17 cases and won 5 times, and the 20 CPU tests trained once and won once.

With full simulator access, agents reused the opponent’s network. In the Privileged Setting the agent can replay the same match in its own simulator. With the GPU, 7 of 10 tests ran reinforcement-learning code and 4 won; with only CPUs, 5 of 10 won. According to their code or final reports, 7 of these 9 wins reused the opponent’s walking network that comes with the simulator. What this one task can and cannot show. The two versions differ at once in the GPU, the training library, the training copies of the match and the instruction, which includes an example of training, so one task and one frozen scene cannot show which difference matters. The same models appear in several settings and harnesses, so the attempts are not independent, and the paper reports these counts without a significance test. Whether an attempt trained a policy was decided from three signals that agree on every Standard and Realtime GPU attempt: calls to a reinforcement-learning library or gradient updates in the agent’s code, the GPU memory recorded by the resource monitor, and the attempt’s final report.

What successful trajectories look like

Solved runs measure before they move: they model the scene and the robot, rehearse offline, and treat a failed episode as data

Four methods recur in the solved runs we read, and they concern measuring the physical world rather than clever code. The agent models the scene from depth: it turns depth images into point clouds and fits the objects it must handle, such as the rings of a rope course or the cells of a bookcase. It builds a local model of the robot, from the robot’s public model or from its own fits of how the robot moves, and tunes its motions there before trying them on the robot. It plans offline and executes in long stretches, sending a motion it has already checked instead of feeling its way step by step. It treats a failed episode as a measurement: where a test allows several episodes, it uses a failure to learn what it could not see, such as a latch that needs a deep press or the flight of a tossed ball.

Other agents use the same methods on tests they solved, according to the paper’s run descriptions. Gemini 4 Argon located a table and a white mug in the point clouds of three calibrated depth cameras in RoboLab, found that the 9 cm mug was wider than the gripper’s largest opening, and grasped the mug’s wall at the rim with one finger inside it. Claude Opus 5.5 estimated a glass jar’s shape and the position of its hinge from the depth point cloud in ManiGuard, swung the lid shut about the hinge and lifted the jar by 1 cm to check the grasp before carrying it to the goal; GPT-6 Astra knocked the same jar over.

The methods do not guarantee that a grasp or contact will hold; they also appear in failed runs. GPT-6 Astra built a local model on the catch-and-shoot test below and ran out of time. Claude Fable 5.2 modeled a pair of interlocked claws from depth and planned their separation offline, but the pieces wedged at waypoint 33 of 47 and its plan had no collision-free next step (run); five of the other nine agents solved that Standard test (GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 4 Argon and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash). Claude Opus 5.5 mapped a wall, its six holes and a coiled rope from depth point clouds on another rope test, but the rope slipped from the gripper whenever the wrist turned, and it threaded none of the holes.

The six runs below are all by Claude Fable 5.2, chosen as clear examples of the methods on tests where GPT-6 Astra failed in the same setting. They are not a sample of all solved runs and do not compare agents overall. Solver counts below come from the site data. Does spending more help?

Spending more is not what buys success: failed attempts cost more than solved ones, and an agent at less than a sixth of the price comes within about two points

Across agents, the cost of an attempt and the chance of success are only loosely related. Within one agent, the expensive attempts are mostly the failures: a failing agent keeps working, and the bill keeps growing. For the five strong agents, the Privileged Setting raises success sharply while lowering the cost per attempt. Money spent mostly tracks how long the agent tried; it does not buy success.

How cost is measured. The paper prices each attempt from the token counts of its API requests at the providers’ list prices (Figure 21), not at what anyone was billed. The 8,918 priced attempts across the ten agents and all settings add up to about $64,530. All cost numbers in this section are from the paper’s cost data.

Price is only a rough guide to success. In the Standard Setting, an attempt of GPT-6 Astra costs $27.12 on average, about twice one of Claude Fable 5.2 ($13.43) and about four times one of Claude Opus 5.5 ($6.45) or Gemini 4 Argon ($6.27). GPT-6.1 Sol costs the least of the five strong agents, $4.10, less than a sixth of Astra’s price, and solves 147 of 456 Standard tests (32.2%) against Astra’s 157 (34.4%), and more than Opus (114) or Argon (110). In the other direction, Kimi 3.1 Pro ($8.05) and Gemini 3.8 Flash ($7.09) spend more per attempt than Opus or Argon and solve less than half as many Standard tests, 46 and 29. Across all three settings, the mean cost per attempt is $24.61 for Astra and $12.48 for Fable.

More expensive agents do tend to solve more: the rank correlation between mean cost and success rate across the ten agents is 0.76 in Standard and 0.64 in Privileged. The examples above show how wide the spread around that trend is.

Per solved test, the order changes again. Total Standard spending divided by tests solved gives the cost of one success: $12.72 for Sol, $13.07 for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, $25.80 for Opus, $26.01 for Argon, $27.09 for Fable, $27.46 for GLM 5.3 Flash, $78.76 for Astra, $79.80 for Kimi 3.1 Pro, $89.89 for Qwen3.8 Max and $111.47 for Gemini 3.8 Flash. A low number does not mean an agent can do the hard tests: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash ($0.40 per Standard attempt) and GLM 5.3 Flash ($0.66) have low costs per success but solve only 14 and 11 of 456.

For every agent, failure is the expensive outcome. In both Standard and Privileged, the median failed attempt costs more than the median solved one, for all ten agents. Standard medians, solved against failed, are $9.01 and $25.90 for Astra, $1.05 and $3.14 for Sol, $8.81 and $12.75 for Fable, $2.97 and $4.66 for Opus, and $2.85 and $5.95 for Argon. Costlier suites do not explain this: within the same suite, the median failed Standard attempt in a typical suite costs 1.29 times (Opus) to 1.92 times (Gemini 3.8 Flash) the median solved one.

Much of the extra cost is extra time, for some agents. 52.5% of Astra’s failed Standard attempts, 54.7% of Sol’s and 78.6% of Argon’s ran to at least 95% of the time limit, against 2.5%, 4.1% and 9.1% of their solved attempts. Only 8.3% of Fable’s and 6.4% of Opus’s failed Standard attempts ran that long, yet their failures also cost more than their successes.

Better information beats more spending. For the five strong agents, a Privileged attempt costs less on average than a Standard one and succeeds far more often: Astra $22.92 against $27.12, Fable $11.36 against $13.43, Sol $3.98 against $4.10, Opus $4.79 against $6.45, and Argon $4.21 against $6.27. What lifts success there is what the agent can know and undo (exact object state, unlimited resets with saved states, a private simulator), not what it spends.

The agents spend an attempt in different ways: Fable writes more and acts less, Astra acts more and runs to the time limit, Opus stops earliest

How long, how many steps and how much output an attempt takes is a second way to look at spending. The table gives these numbers for three agents in each setting (a step is one turn of the agent with its tool calls; the time limit is each test’s own). Three patterns hold in all three settings. Fable writes the most output per attempt (about 124k to 146k tokens, against about 45k to 55k for Astra) and takes the fewest steps in Standard and Privileged. Astra takes the most steps and is the only agent that often runs into the time limit (92 Standard and 45 Privileged attempts). Opus is the cheapest of the three and ends its failed attempts earliest: a median 45% of the time limit in Standard, against 67% for Fable and 97% for Astra.

The table also qualifies the early stopping discussed in the first part. Leaving time on the clock is not unique to Fable: Astra also ends 133 of its 299 failed Standard attempts with ten minutes or more left, and Opus 305 of 342. And where an attempt succeeds, Fable is not the faster agent: its solved Standard attempts take a median 33 minutes, against 23 for Astra and 24 for Opus.

Thinking harder changes how GPT-6.1 Sol works but hardly changes how often it succeeds

Another way to spend more is to let the model think longer before each action. Every leaderboard agent runs at high reasoning effort; the paper also ran GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex on the 456 core Standard tests at medium and at maximum effort, with everything else as in the leaderboard run.

The three efforts are within run noise of each other. Medium, high and maximum effort solve 30.7%, 32.2% and 30.9%, a spread of 1.5 points. That is smaller than the spread between three high-effort runs of Sol on the same tests (147, 147 and 127 solved; mean 30.8%, standard deviation 2.5 points; see run variance). Nothing suggests that another effort would have scored meaningfully differently from the leaderboard’s high effort.

What changes is the way the agent works. With more effort, Sol writes more output tokens, sends fewer requests and runs longer: it thinks more before each step and takes fewer, slower steps. Both styles solve about the same share of these physical tasks.

The cost stays about the same, $3.91 to $4.10 per attempt, probably because fewer requests offset the extra output tokens. The paper does not break the cost down further, so this is our reading, not a measured result.

How the harness affects results

An agent’s score belongs to the model and its harness together: the same model moves by up to 7.5 points when the harness changes

Every agent in this exam is a model running inside a harness: the program that hands the model its tools, keeps its conversation, and decides when a session ends. Claude Fable 5.2, Claude Opus 5.5 and three of the weaker models (Kimi 3.1 Pro, Qwen3.8 Max and GLM 5.3 Flash) run in Claude Code, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex, the two Gemini models in Antigravity CLI, and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash in DeepSeek Harness, DeepSeek’s own. To measure the harness’s share, the paper ran three models in other harnesses on the same 456 core Standard tests, with the leaderboard’s time limits and reasoning effort (Figure 16c). The effect ranges from 7.5 points to almost nothing, and a model’s own harness is not always its best.

GPT-6 Astra loses 7.5 points outside Codex. It solves 157 of 456 tests (34.4%) in Codex, its own harness, and 123 (27.0%) in Claude Code. Single tests move both ways: 75 are solved only in Codex and 41 only in Claude Code. The paper finds the difference significant (paired exact test, p = 0.002, uncorrected for multiple comparisons). Most of it is already there in the first episode, before any reset: 119 successes in Codex against 95 (p = 0.015, uncorrected). So the gap does not come only from how the harnesses handle long sessions with many retries.

Claude Opus 5.5 is about the same in both. It solves 119 of 456 in Codex and 114 in its own Claude Code, and 97 against 96 in the first episode. Underneath, 35 tests are solved only in Codex and 30 only in Claude Code. Neither count differs significantly.

Gemini 4 Argon does best in another harness. It solves 132 of 456 in Codex, 116 in Claude Code and 114 in its own Antigravity CLI. Against its own harness, Codex wins 46 tests and loses 28, a net 18 (p = 0.047, uncorrected, so only nominally significant), and BEHAVIOR-1K contributes 6 of the 18, the most of any suite. Claude Code and Antigravity CLI are close (p = 0.92). The first-episode order is the same, 114, 109 and 100, but the Codex advantage there is not significant (p = 0.11).

Two caveats. First, Codex and Claude Code speak only their own provider’s API format. In the cross-provider runs, an adapter passes each request to the other provider, translates the answer back, and adapts the system prompt and model metadata. The paper says these runs only approximate a native setup, so part of a drop in a foreign harness may come from the translation rather than the harness design. Second, Argon’s BEHAVIOR-1K tests were rerun in Antigravity CLI with the current instruction so that all three harnesses share it, which is why its bar there is 114 rather than the leaderboard’s 110 (Figure 8); its other Antigravity CLI results are from the leaderboard run. The harness runs are reported separately and do not change the leaderboard.

So when one agent solves a test that another fails, part of the difference can come from the harness. The paper therefore reports every result for a model in a particular harness: how the harness exposes tools, manages the context and ends a session is part of what the exam measures.

How a harness ends a session and summarizes a long one can decide whether a physical task gets finished

Time limits in this exam run from 30 minutes to four hours, and a failing agent often works until the clock stops. Two harness choices that rarely matter in a short coding task matter here: what the harness does when the model provider refuses a reply, and what it keeps when the conversation outgrows the model’s context. The data show clear differences between harnesses on both, but not how much of the score gap each one explains.

Ending the session on a blocked reply turns a recoverable hiccup into a failed attempt. In 162 attempts across the exam, the provider’s content filter stopped the session, and the attempt ended or submitted nothing: 153 from Gemini 4 Argon, 7 from Gemini 3.8 Flash, and one each from Claude Fable 5.2 and Qwen3.8 Max. A likely reason it lands almost entirely on the Gemini models is that their harness, Antigravity CLI, ends the session after a blocked reply, whereas Codex sends the request again and Claude Code asks the model to continue. The paper does not measure how much of the pattern this explains, since the filters may also block different models at different rates. Argon’s cutoffs fall in every setting: 36 in Standard, 9 in Realtime and 108 in Privileged. In Privileged, where the agent must submit a recorded trajectory, 79 of Argon’s 134 attempts that never submitted one were filter cutoffs.

These attempts count as failures on the leaderboard. The paper removes them only from the failure-stage statistics, because a filter cutoff says little about what went wrong physically. The data cannot tell whether Argon would have solved these tests with retries, and the paper does not count how many of Argon’s harness-comparison failures ended on a blocked reply, so we do not claim that the cutoffs explain its gap to Codex.

Each harness compacts a long conversation differently. When the context fills, the harness replaces the conversation with a summary. Antigravity CLI does so at about 260k tokens and keeps roughly the first 10k characters of the task instruction; Codex does so at about 240k tokens and keeps the full instruction; Claude Code keeps a summary and the most recent messages. Running Gemini 4 Argon on the 456 core Standard tests, both Antigravity CLI and Codex compacted in 287 tests, one of them in 63, and neither in 106.

Argon’s gap between the two harnesses sits in the long, compacted tests. Where neither compacted, Antigravity CLI is slightly ahead, 64 tests against 60. Where only one compacted, they are even, 18 against 19. Where both compacted, Codex solves 53 and Antigravity CLI 32, and 33 of Codex’s 53 are tests that Antigravity CLI fails (12 go the other way). Failures in these tests tend to run out the clock: 209 of Antigravity CLI’s 255 failed attempts and 174 of Codex’s 234 ran until the time limit.

After compaction, Antigravity CLI’s commands fail about twice as often. In attempts that compacted, the share of shell commands that exit with an error rises from 7.7% before the first compaction to 16.2% after it in Antigravity CLI, and from 8.3% to 11.9% in Codex. The paper tests whether the agent forgets instruction details the summary dropped, and finds that this does not appear to account for the gap. Outside BEHAVIOR-1K (whose attempts all lost less than 30% of the instruction), in the 117 tests where Antigravity CLI dropped less than 30% of the instruction it solves 14 and Codex 27; in the 133 tests where it dropped 30% or more, it solves 20 and Codex 23. The gap is smaller where more was dropped, the opposite of what forgetting predicts. And in both harnesses, only about a quarter of the missing names in lookup errors after compaction (23.5% in Antigravity CLI, 23.1% in Codex) appear in the instruction at all. The paper calls all of this an association, not a cause.

What harness builders can check. These are things to check, not proven fixes:

  • Do not end a long session on one refused reply. With an hour or more left, ending the session throws away the remaining time; retrying (Codex) or asking the model to continue (Claude Code) keeps the attempt alive. Here this choice put 153 attempts of one agent out of the physical failure analysis.
  • Measure compaction as a point where the agent can lose its footing. A simple signal is the share of commands that fail before and after each compaction; in Argon’s runs, the harness with the larger rise solved fewer of the long tests. Which part of the summary matters is not settled: keeping the full instruction alone does not seem to be it.
  • Test a harness on long tasks. The two harnesses were about even until tests ran into the context limit and, often, the time limit.
  • Report the harness next to the model. It can move a model’s score by several points.

Does the task instruction change the result?

A case from RoboDojo: stating the success check in full triples the score

RoboDojo, a two-arm manipulation benchmark that the exam adapts, gives each task a one-sentence instruction, but its success check tests conditions the sentence does not mention. Given a goal that states the check in full, the same agent’s score roughly triples. The difference lies in whether the agent knows what will be graded, not in the robot skill being tested.

How the exam uses RoboDojo. The exam keeps RoboDojo’s scenes, robots and success rules, and writes an instruction that states every convention the agent cannot infer by looking. “Fill Egg Holder”, for example, asks the agent to move four eggs into an egg holder, close its lid, “and return the arm home” (from the site’s task list). Twelve RoboDojo tasks are in the core set, each with a 60-minute limit and a Standard and a Privileged test (no Realtime test).

The one-sentence instructions leave out what the check tests. The official first-exam report gave GPT-6 Astra all 54 RoboDojo tasks in the Standard Setting with three versions of the goal text, keeping the scenes, step limits and success checks the same. RoboDojo’s own instructions omit conditions its check tests, above all that both arms end near their start pose, which 47 of the 54 tasks require; an agent that does the visible task perfectly and leaves an arm hovering over the table fails. These 54-task counts come from the first-exam report, not the paper’s core-set tables:

The largest jump, from 12 to 33 of 54, comes from adding the recipe and the start-pose note: mostly information about what the check wants, although the scoring stages also spell out the steps of the task. Stating the check in full adds 4 more, to 37. Robot, scene and agent are the same in all three rows, so a score of 12 of 54 would have measured how well the instruction matched the check far more than how well Astra manipulates objects with two arms.

With the full goal, RoboDojo still separates agents, in both directions. On the 12 core RoboDojo tests in the Standard Setting, Claude Fable 5.2 solves 6 (50%), Gemini 4 Argon 4, GPT-6 Astra 3 (25%), Claude Opus 5.5 2, Kimi 3.1 Pro 1, and GPT-6.1 Sol and the other four weaker agents none (site data). The solved sets are not nested. Only Argon and Fable solve the randomized sweeping task in Standard (Sweep Blocks Random: hand a broom to the other arm and sweep small objects into a dustpan), which Astra fails, and the block-swapping test is solved in Standard by Kimi 3.1 Pro alone, none of the five strong agents included. In Privileged the counts rise to 11 for Fable and 10 for Astra, with Sol at 6, Opus 3, Argon 3 and Kimi 2. Sol goes from none in Standard to 6 in Privileged, while Argon goes the other way, 4 in Standard and 3 in Privileged.

With the full goal, what remains is real physical difficulty. In one Standard attempt at inserting a key, Claude Opus 5.5 located the key and the lock from depth images but dropped the key at least nine times during hand-overs and large joint moves; its most accurate insertion, at minute 36, was about 9 mm off and hit the face of the lock (paper appendix). No agent solves that test in Standard, while Fable, Astra and Argon solve it in Privileged (site data).

The lesson for benchmark design. If the success check tests a condition the instruction does not state, the benchmark measures whether the agent guessed that condition rather than the ability it was built to measure. Two rules follow: state every condition the check will test, including “return to the start pose” and similar housekeeping; and when comparing results across papers, check which instruction text each used. On RoboDojo, the text alone moves one agent from 12 to 37 of 54.

What’s next

Limitations

Every comparison here comes from one attempt per agent and test; three runs of GPT-6.1 Sol in the Standard Setting give a success rate of 30.8% ± 2.5 (see Run to run). The stage labels behind Part 3 come from a labeling pipeline that combines labeling by coding agents (GPT-6.1 Sol and Claude Sonnet 5.5) with human spot checks while the prompts were developed, and the control-approach labels behind Part 4 were written by an agent. All results except one real-robot demonstration are in simulation. The paper discusses these and further limits, including possible memorization of public benchmarks and how the core set was screened (paper, Limitations).

A hidden set of tasks, so that results measure ability rather than memory

Because results on tasks adapted from public benchmarks may partly reflect what a model has seen before, we are building a hidden set of held-out tasks that will not be released. Agents will be evaluated on it under the same settings and the same external verifier, so a result there shows how an agent handles a task it could not have seen.

Evaluation on real robots, starting from the same Standard interface

The Standard Setting gives the agent only the robot’s sensor readings and control interface, so the same interface can drive a physical robot once the outcome can be graded. We are extending the exam to real robots, with tasks and protocols that let a real-robot attempt be run and graded consistently.

As a first test on hardware, the authors connected GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 to a physical robot arm playing a buzz wire game (paper Figure 34). The robot holds the handle of a metal loop and must guide it along a bent wire fixed to a wooden board without touching it. Loop and wire form one electric circuit, so any contact turns on a light and a buzzer and ends the trial, as in RoboARC’s simulated buzz wire tasks on a Franka Panda arm. The agent sends discrete commands: move the gripper up, down, left or right, or rotate it either way. Each agent ran eight trials in a row on the same wire, each scored by progress, the share of the wire the loop covered before the trial ended (100% is the whole wire without a contact).

Both agents scored higher in the last four trials than in the first four: average progress rose from about 65% to about 94% for Astra and from about 35% to about 63% for Opus. Astra started at about 60%, dipped once (from about 80% in trial 3 to about 60% in trial 4), and covered the whole wire in trials 7 and 8. Opus scored 0% in trial 1, then swung between about 40% and 80% (about 80% in trial 6, 40% in trial 7, 80% in trial 8) and never covered the whole wire. Astra scored higher in every trial and reached at least 80% in five of its eight trials, Opus in two.

The paper keeps the demonstration out of every success rate. It covers one task, one robot and two agents with one sequence of eight trials each, no program grades the trials, and Claude Fable 5.2 was not tested. It shows that a coding agent can control a physical robot arm in this game, not how well simulation results predict hardware results.

More runs per test, and more tasks where the clock keeps running

Running every agent several times, as the paper did for GPT-6.1 Sol (Run to run), will separate failures an agent repeats from failures of a single run, and will give confidence intervals for the comparisons between agents and between settings in this post. The paper also plans more Realtime tasks, since the Realtime Setting has the largest share of tests that no agent solves.
