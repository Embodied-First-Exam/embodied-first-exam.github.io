GPT-6.1 Sol

GPT-6.1 Sol is third in every setting of the exam, at the lowest cost per attempt of the strong agents. What does it solve, and where does it fail?

November 2026 · The report · Leaderboard · Claude Fable 5.2

With only the robot’s sensors it solves 32.2% of the tests, ten fewer than GPT-6 Astra, for less than a sixth of its cost per attempt.

We ran GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex on the core set of the Embodied First Exam, with the same tasks, settings and limits as the other nine agents of the report: one attempt per test, at high reasoning effort. It is the tenth agent on the leaderboard and third in every setting, behind Claude Fable 5.2 and GPT-6 Astra. It solves 147 of the 456 Standard tests (32.2%), 281 of the 391 Privileged tests (71.9%) and 13 of the 54 Realtime tests (24.1%), and 132 of the 456 core tasks Overall (28.9%). This post reports its results, analyses where and why it fails, describes how it works, and follows several of its runs in full. Two more runs at high effort and two runs at other reasoning efforts close the post as supplementary results.

In short, it runs in the same harness as GPT-6 Astra, and the two are close: no suite separates them by more than five tests, and each solves dozens of tests the other fails. An attempt costs less than a sixth of one of GPT-6 Astra. Like GPT-6 Astra, it keeps little more than a third of its Privileged successes when it has only the robot’s sensors, where Claude Fable 5.2 keeps more than half. Its most frequent failure is a lost hold, and per grasp it loses its hold more often than any other strong agent.

Data as of , the same results as the leaderboard: all the core tests of each setting and each agent’s counted attempt. GPT-6.1 Sol’s results are those of the report. The settings are defined in the report. Every run named below opens step by step on the runs page. Each agent made one attempt per test, so differences of a few tests, in a small suite or in the Realtime Setting, may come from the variation between runs; the supplementary results at the end measure it for GPT-6.1 Sol. 02Failure analysis of GPT-6.1 Sol

How it fails

We divide each task into stages, each a change in the world that the task requires: putting a tomato into a closed fridge has three, the fridge open, the tomato inside and the fridge closed again. For each failed attempt, a coding agent labels, from the replay video and the logs, the stage at which the attempt went wrong and what visibly went wrong there, such as a lost hold or a fall, and a second agent reviews the labels. We labeled 852 attempts of GPT-6.1 Sol on 427 tasks in the same way as those of the other nine agents in the report. The writer of these labels is GPT-6.1 Sol itself, the model that wrote most of the report’s labels; Claude Sonnet 5.5, a different model, reviewed every label. An agent that labels its own attempts, and that belongs to the same family as GPT-6 Astra, could bias the comparison. The labels come from the replay video and the logs, without the simulator state, so a wrong estimate and a wrong motion often cannot be told apart. Figure 6 compares GPT-6.1 Sol with GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Opus 5.5.

It loses its hold more than any other agent. A lost hold appears in 20.2% of its failed attempts, the highest share of all ten agents. For Claude Fable 5.2 a high share came with the lowest rate per grasp, because it reaches more stages that need a grasp; GPT-6.1 Sol’s rate per grasp stage reached, 7.0%, is also the highest of the five strong agents (GPT-6 Astra 5.4%, Claude Opus 5.5 6.5%), though below the 8.1% to 9.8% of the weaker agents.

It makes contact like a strong agent, and fails more often before it gets there. Counted per contact stage that it reaches, it fails without contact at 3.4% of these stages, within the 1.0% to 3.7% of the strong agents and far below the 9.0% to 18.5% of the weaker ones. Yet 12.5% of its failed attempts fail to make a required contact, and 31% of its failed Standard attempts meet no stage of the task, both more than for GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2.

When both fail, GPT-6 Astra usually gets further. On the tests with at least three stages that both failed, GPT-6 Astra met more stages than GPT-6.1 Sol on 75% of 79, leaving out ties, against 56% of 72 for Claude Fable 5.2. Four tests from the report show where its attempts stopped.

03How GPT-6.1 Sol works

A separate coding agent read the logs of 289 attempts of GPT-6.1 Sol, sampled at random with the same rule as the report’s control-approach sample, and labeled the approach that produced each graded answer. In the Standard Setting it mostly steers the robot directly, step by step through the robot’s interface: 104 of 145 sampled attempts (72%), against 32 that run a program. With privileged access it mostly writes programs (84 of 127), but it still steers directly in 34, or 27%, against 9% for the other agents in the report’s sample. Nineteen of these 34 are BEHAVIOR-1K tasks, in which it steps a live session with motion primitives, reads the privileged state and records the actions as its answer, as GPT-6 Astra did in nearly all of its sampled BEHAVIOR-1K attempts. In the Realtime Setting, 16 of its 17 sampled attempts run a program. It also sends many requests: 185 per Standard attempt on average, as the report gives.

It trains policies when the test gives it the means. In two robot soccer matches at the joint level, a team of two humanoids against a learned opponent, it trained its own walking networks: on the GPU with privileged access, where it won, and on six CPUs in the Standard Setting, where it lost.

Trained on the GPU, won 6–3GPT-6.1 Sol · solvedPrivileged · GPU · minute 147 of 240It saved a standing policy first so that an answer existed, then trained a fresh walking gait in the supplied GPU simulator and a ball-contact skill from its own checkpoint, and wrote the team tactics separately. Its own full match against the level-5 opponent ended 6–3, with a matching replay, and the test passed. Trained on six CPUs, lost 1–4GPT-6.1 Sol · failedStandard · CPU only · minute 143 of 240It kept a hand-built balance controller as a baseline and trained a walking policy from scratch with reinforcement learning, at about 11,000 control steps per second. The policy walked and turned, and its practice losses narrowed, but in the match it trailed 1–4 and then missed the five-minute review deadline, which ended the match as a loss. Of the soccer attempts with only CPUs in the Standard and Realtime settings, the report finds only this one trained a policy with reinforcement learning.

05Supplementary: repeated runs and reasoning effort

The leaderboard counts one attempt per agent and test. To measure how much a result changes from one run to the next, and how much it depends on the reasoning effort, we ran GPT-6.1 Sol in Codex on the 456 core Standard tests two more times at high effort, and once each at medium and at maximum effort, as the report’s appendix describes. None of these runs counts on the leaderboard.

The success rate changes by a few points from run to run, but the set of solved tests changes much more. The three runs at high effort solve 147, 147 and 124 tests, and each by itself would rank third in the Standard column. Yet only 69 tests are solved in all three runs, and of the tests that one run solves, another run solves only 56% to 70%, about the share of GPT-6.1 Sol’s solved tests that GPT-6 Astra solves. Counting a test as solved when any of the three runs solves it gives 47.1%, close to Claude Fable 5.2’s 49.6% in its one run (Figure 9).

The reasoning effort changes how it works more than how often it succeeds. At medium, high and maximum effort it solves within 2.4 percentage points of each other, less than the three runs at high effort differ, and an attempt costs about the same. With more effort it writes more output tokens, sends fewer requests and runs longer (Figure 10). The medium- and maximum-effort runs open on the runs page as “effort comparison” (medium, maximum).
